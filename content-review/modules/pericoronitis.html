Clinical Urgent M15

Pericoronitis

Not every wisdom tooth infection needs admission—but every wisdom tooth infection deserves a careful assessment.

#pericoronitis#wisdom-teeth#odontogenic-infection#trismus#dental-emergency
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

Pericoronitis is one of the commonest reasons for emergency dental and OMFS attendance. 80–90% involve mandibular third molars in young adults. Most episodes are mild and managed as outpatient with local measures. However, infection can spread beyond the operculum into fascial spaces, causing trismus, cellulitis, deep neck infection and airway compromise. The OMFS DCT must distinguish simple pericoronitis from spreading odontogenic infection.

Golden Rule

Treat the patient, not the wisdom tooth. The presence of an inflamed operculum is less important than the patient's airway, systemic condition and extent of infection.

Core Content

What Is Pericoronitis?

Inflammation/infection of soft tissues around crown of partially erupted tooth. Most commonly mandibular thirds, 18–30 years. Food/plaque accumulates under operculum → anaerobic bacterial growth. Recurrent attacks common unless cause addressed.

Risk Factors

Partially erupted lower 8, poor OH, food stagnation, opposing upper 8 trauma, smoking, stress, recent URTI, pregnancy.

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Diagnosis often obvious, but start with ABCDE. Assess airway: voice change, difficulty swallowing, floor of mouth swelling, respiratory distress. These are NOT typical of uncomplicated pericoronitis and suggest spreading infection.

History

Presenting complaint: Pain around wisdom tooth, swollen gum, difficulty chewing, bad taste, difficulty opening mouth.

HPC: Onset sudden/gradual? First episode or recurrent? Improving/worsening? Severity, radiation to ear, pain on biting, night pain. Functional: difficulty opening mouth, swallowing, reduced intake, speech changes.

Previous episodes: “Keeps happening every few months” = indication for definitive treatment.

Examination

General: Comfortable, in pain, septic? Temp, pulse, NEWS2.

Extraoral: Facial swelling, cervical lymphadenopathy, tenderness, trismus. Measure mouth opening.

Intraoral: Partially erupted tooth, inflamed operculum, pus, food debris, ulceration, opposing upper 8 trauma, oral hygiene. Palpate gently for tenderness/drainage.

Typical Clinical Features

Pain around partially erupted lower 8, inflamed operculum, halitosis, bad taste, local swelling, mild trismus, tender cervical nodes. These alone do not indicate severe infection.

Red Flags ⚠️

Escalate if: dysphagia, voice change, bilateral submandibular swelling, raised floor of mouth, significant facial swelling, mouth opening <20mm, sepsis, rapid progression, immunocompromised. Think beyond pericoronitis → spreading odontogenic infection.

Investigations

Bloods: Usually unnecessary in uncomplicated cases. Consider FBC, CRP, U&E if systemically unwell, admission likely, significant swelling, concern re spreading infection.

OPG: Investigation of choice. Assess tooth position, impaction, IAN relationship, caries, distal caries in 7, associated pathology.

CT: Rarely required. Indications: significant facial swelling, neck swelling, deep space infection, diagnostic uncertainty, abscess concern.

Management

Mild: Irrigation under operculum with saline/chlorhexidine, remove food debris, OHI, analgesia, warm saline rinses. Antibiotics NOT routinely indicated.

Moderate: Local debridement, OHI, analgesia, antibiotics only if spreading infection/systemic involvement per local guidelines.

Severe: Trismus, facial swelling, cellulitis, dysphagia, systemic illness → admit, IV antibiotics per guidelines, imaging if indicated, urgent reg discussion.

Should Tooth Be Extracted Acutely?

Exam question. In selected cases, extraction acutely is appropriate and provides immediate source control if access adequate and operator experienced. However, severe trismus, extensive cellulitis, limited access may make immediate extraction unsafe. Priority is controlling acute infection before definitive treatment. Decision individualised.

Definitive Management

Recurrent pericoronitis, repeated antibiotics, recurrent pain/swelling, caries/periodontal disease → assess for removal of 8 per NICE/accepted guidance.

Admission Criteria

Airway concerns, significant trismus, dysphagia, facial cellulitis, deep space infection, IV antibiotics needed, significant comorbidity, failed outpatient management.

Key Takeaways

Most pericoronitis is local inflammatory process, but exclude spreading infection first

Antibiotics only if systemic involvement/spreading cellulitis — local measures sufficient for many

Recurrent pericoronitis usually requires definitive management of offending 8

If dysphagia, significant trismus, or facial swelling develops, reassess urgently

Registrar Handover Prompt

Know: which tooth, airway status, mouth opening, facial swelling, pus, dysphagia, obs, OPG findings, whether admission needed.
