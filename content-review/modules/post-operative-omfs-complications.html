Post-operative OMFS Complications

The operation may have finished, but your responsibility has not. The first signs of a complication are often recognised by the junior doctor. Early recognition and escalation prevent small problems becoming major ones.

Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Module Matters

Most OMFS patients recover uneventfully, but every DCT will review post-op concerns on the ward, in ED, or by phone. The challenge is distinguishing expected recovery — pain, swelling peaking 48–72h, bruising, trismus, minor oozing — from developing complications. You are often first to see the patient. Your assessment, documentation, and timely escalation prevent serious morbidity. Remember: “When reviewing a post-operative patient, always ask yourself: Is this the expected post-operative course, or is something changing?”

Golden Rule

Never reassure yourself because the patient had surgery yesterday. Reassure yourself because you have examined them thoroughly today.

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Always begin with ABCDE. Many serious post-op complications present with airway compromise, haemorrhage, sepsis, aspiration, or acute pain. Treat physiological deterioration before focusing on surgical site.

The Expected Post-operative Course

Common and usually normal: mild-moderate pain, facial swelling maximal 48–72h, bruising, limited mouth opening, minor oozing, temporary altered sensation, reduced oral intake. These alone are not complications.

Red Flags 🚨

Immediate senior review if: rapidly increasing swelling, airway compromise, active haemorrhage, difficulty swallowing saliva, fever + systemic deterioration, purulent wound discharge, new cranial nerve deficit, sudden visual disturbance, chest pain/SOB, persistent hypotension or tachycardia.

Common Post-operative Problems

1. Haemorrhage

Ask: Is bleeding active? Has patient swallowed blood? Airway threatened? What procedure?

Management: ABCDE, apply pressure where appropriate, assess haemodynamics, IV access, FBC + clotting + G&S/crossmatch, escalate immediately. Never underestimate concealed bleeding into floor of mouth or neck.

2. Haematoma

Features: progressive swelling, tension, bruising, pain. Large neck haematomas may compromise airway. Urgent senior review. May need return to theatre for evacuation.

3. Surgical Site Infection

Usually develops days after surgery. Look for: increasing pain after initial improvement, erythema, purulent discharge, fever, tenderness, wound breakdown. Management: antibiotics per local guidance, drainage, microbiology sampling, consider imaging if deep collection suspected.

4. Wound Dehiscence

Assess: size, depth, bone/hardware exposure, infection signs. Small mucosal dehiscence may heal conservatively. Larger defects or exposure require senior assessment.

5. Facial Swelling

Expected: gradual ↑ 48–72h, soft, minimal erythema. Concerning: rapid expansion, tense swelling, airway symptoms, severe pain, fever. Consider haematoma, infection, angioedema.

6. Pain Out of Proportion

Severe/worsening pain prompts reassessment. Consider: infection, haematoma, compartment-type pressure, hardware problems, missed diagnosis. Do not just prescribe stronger analgesia without examining.

7. Trismus

Mild common. Worsening trismus may indicate infection, haematoma, deep space collection, TMJ pathology. Measure inter-incisal opening, compare to previous.

8. Nerve Deficits

Document carefully: IAN, lingual, infraorbital, facial nerve. Ask: present immediately post-op? Changed? Complete/partial? New/progressive facial nerve weakness after parotid surgery = urgent consultant review.

9. Hardware Problems

Complaints: mobility, clicking, pain, wound breakdown, plate exposure. Do not manipulate fixation. Image after senior discussion.

10. Airway Compromise

The complication you must never miss. Warning signs: stridor, drooling, voice change, difficulty swallowing, tongue elevation, increasing neck swelling. Call for senior help immediately. Prepare for difficult airway.

Investigations

Targeted to clinical question:

Bloods: FBC, CRP, U&E, clotting, blood cultures if septic.

Imaging: CT with contrast for suspected deep space collection/haematoma, OPG/plain films for hardware, USS for superficial collections.

Assessing the Surgical Site

Ask: Is wound clean? Sutures intact? Discharge? Tissue necrosis? Is swelling expected? Does appearance match symptoms? Compare with operation note if possible.

Reviewing the Operation Note

Before calling consultant, read: indication, findings, difficulties, drains placed, expected post-op instructions. Many questions are answered here.

Clinical Pearl – The Consultant's Question

If asked: "Why have you called me?"

Avoid: "The patient doesn't look right."

Instead: "This patient is 6h post-ORIF mandible fracture. They’ve developed rapidly increasing submandibular swelling, dysphagia and tachycardia 120 bpm. I'm concerned about expanding haematoma with potential airway compromise and think they need urgent senior review."

Consultants value clear clinical reasoning over certainty.
