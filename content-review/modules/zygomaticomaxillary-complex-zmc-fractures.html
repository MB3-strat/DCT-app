Zygomaticomaxillary Complex (ZMC) Fractures

A flattened cheek, infraorbital numbness and diplopia should immediately make you think of a zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture. Always assess function before deciding on treatment.

Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

The zygomaticomaxillary complex, often called the cheekbone, is one of the most frequently fractured regions of the facial skeleton. Common after assaults, falls, sporting injuries and RTCs. Although many ZMC fractures appear dramatic due to swelling, your job is not simply to identify the fracture on CT. The real priorities are determining whether the injury has affected vision, eye movements, globe position, infraorbital nerve function, mouth opening, and facial symmetry. Many minimally displaced fractures are managed conservatively. Displaced fractures causing functional or cosmetic deformity usually require surgery.

Golden Rule

Always examine the eye before examining the CT scan. A perfectly reduced fracture is of little value if an ocular injury has been missed.

Anatomy You Need to Understand

The zygoma forms the prominence of the cheek and contributes to: lateral orbital wall, orbital floor, zygomatic arch, anterior maxillary buttress. A ZMC fracture usually involves separation at four articulations: frontozygomatic suture, zygomaticomaxillary buttress, zygomaticotemporal articulation (arch), orbital floor/lateral orbital wall. You don’t need to identify all four clinically, but understanding this explains why displacement affects both facial contour and orbital anatomy.

Initial Assessment – ATLS Principles

Always ABCDE before focusing on the face. Patients with sufficient force to fracture the zygoma may also have: head injury, C-spine injury, mandibular fractures, orbital injuries, thoracic trauma.

History

Mechanism: Assault, sport, fall, RTC? Direct blows to cheek typical.

Symptoms: Cheek pain, facial swelling, flattening of cheek, double vision, numb upper lip/cheek, difficulty opening mouth, pain on chewing, cosmetic asymmetry.

Visual Symptoms: Always ask: Is vision blurred? Double vision? Is one eye sitting differently? Any flashes or floaters? Visual symptoms require careful assessment.

Examination

Inspection: Look from front and above. Assess: facial asymmetry, loss of malar prominence, periorbital bruising, subconjunctival haemorrhage (especially without visible posterior limit), eyelid swelling, lacerations. Flattened cheek may become more obvious as swelling settles.

The Eye Examination — Mandatory in every patient. Document every component.

Visual Acuity: Test each eye separately. Document formally. Never omit. If no chart: reading print, counting fingers, hand movements, or perception of light.

Pupils: Size, equality, direct/consensual responses, RAPD. RAPD = important sign of optic nerve injury.

Eye Movements: Assess all directions. Restricted upward gaze may indicate orbital floor involvement or muscle entrapment. Significant displacement.

Diplopia: Ask specifically: “Do you see double?” Determine if constant, only on upward gaze, or in primary gaze. Diplopia in primary gaze has greater functional significance.

Visual Fields: Confrontation testing if possible.

Globe Position: Assess enophthalmos, proptosis, vertical dystopia. Compare both eyes from above.

Red Flags 🚩

Urgent senior review + ophthalmology if: reduced visual acuity, RAPD, suspected globe rupture, retrobulbar haemorrhage, progressive proptosis, severe restriction of eye movements, entrapment particularly in children, hyphaema. These are true emergencies.

Facial Examination

Palpation: Infraorbital rim, zygomatic arch, frontozygomatic region, zygomaticomaxillary buttress. Look for tenderness, step deformities, mobility, crepitus.

Mouth Opening: Reduced opening may occur because fractured zygomatic arch impinges on coronoid process, or due to pain. Measure inter-incisal opening.

Infraorbital Nerve: Test sensation of lower eyelid, cheek, side of nose, upper lip. Reduced sensation common but may recover gradually over months. Document carefully.

Investigations

CT Facial Bones: Investigation of choice. Assess: degree of displacement, orbital floor involvement, zygomatic arch, frontozygomatic suture, maxillary buttress, associated facial fractures. Review CT yourself before discussing with registrar.

Conservative Management

Many minimally displaced fractures managed without surgery. Typical features: normal facial contour, no diplopia, no significant enophthalmos, minimal displacement, adequate mouth opening. Management: analgesia, soft diet, avoidance of further trauma, facial trauma clinic follow-up.

Operative Management

Common indications: significant cosmetic flattening, persistent diplopia due to fracture displacement, clinically significant enophthalmos, reduced mouth opening from arch impingement, marked displacement, associated orbital reconstruction. Most repairs = ORIF with titanium plates/screws. Number/position of fixation points depends on pattern/stability.

Orbital Floor Involvement

Many ZMC fractures include orbital floor component. Surgical repair may be required if: persistent diplopia, muscle entrapment, significant enophthalmos, large orbital floor defect. Always discuss orbital findings with registrar.

Antibiotics

Routine antibiotics NOT indicated for isolated closed ZMC fractures. Follow local protocols if: open fractures, associated sinus injuries, surgical intervention.

Admission

Consider if: significant ocular injury, associated facial fractures, poor oral intake, uncontrolled pain, requirement for surgery, social concerns.

Discharge Advice

Advise: soft diet if chewing painful, avoid contact sports until reviewed, avoid further facial trauma, attend follow-up, return immediately if vision deteriorates or new diplopia develops. If orbital floor fracture present, advise NOT to blow nose — may precipitate orbital emphysema.

Key Takeaways

Every ZMC fracture requires documented eye exam — vision, movements, diplopia, globe position

Assess infraorbital nerve function in every case — medicolegal and prognostic importance

Restricted mouth opening may indicate zygomatic arch impingement — not just pain/trismus

Operate on function and deformity, not simply CT appearance — displacement alone isn’t indication

Vision always takes priority over fracture fixation — missed ocular injury is never acceptable
