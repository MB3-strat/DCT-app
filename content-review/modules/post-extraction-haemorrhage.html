Post-Extraction Haemorrhage

Most post-extraction bleeding can be controlled with simple measures. The skill is recognising when simple measures are no longer enough.

#post-extraction-bleeding#haemorrhage#anticoagulants#local-haemostasis#emergency#dental-complication
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

Post-extraction haemorrhage is one of the commonest emergency OMFS presentations. Most patients arrive anxious, frightened and convinced they are "bleeding to death", when in reality they have persistent oozing mixed with saliva. However, a small number have significant haemorrhage requiring prompt intervention, particularly those on anticoagulants, with bleeding disorders, or after difficult surgical extractions. Your role: assess patient not just socket, determine if true haemorrhage or blood-stained saliva, identify cause, achieve haemostasis, recognise when senior help required. Calm, systematic approach manages vast majority.

Golden Rule

Pressure stops most bleeding. Panic stops nothing. Before reaching for haemostatic agents or calling theatre, ensure effective local pressure has been applied correctly.

Core Content

Understanding Post-Extraction Haemorrhage

Primary: During procedure. Managed immediately.

Reactionary: Within 24h. Most common presentation to OMFS. Due to loss of vasoconstrictor, rising BP, inadequate clot, clot dislodgement.

Secondary: Several days later. Usually infection, clot breakdown, delayed healing. Much less common.

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Airway: Ensure clear. Large volumes swallowed → nausea/vomiting. Profuse bleeding: sit upright, spit into bowl not swallow.

Breathing: RR, SpO2, associated chest injuries.

Circulation: Priority. Document pulse, BP, CRT, skin colour. Ask: "Have you felt dizzy or faint?" "How many gauzes/towels soaked?" "Have you fainted?" Persistent tachycardia/hypotension → urgent senior review.

Disability: GCS, symptoms of hypovolaemia.

Exposure: Estimate blood loss, ongoing bleeding, bruising elsewhere.

History

Presenting Complaint: Is bleeding continuous? Did it stop and restart? Gradually worsened?

Dental History: Which tooth removed? Simple or surgical? Bone removed? Sutures placed? Bleeding difficult during extraction?

Medical History: Anticoagulants (DOACs/warfarin), antiplatelets, liver disease, renal disease, bleeding disorders, previous prolonged bleeding, chemo, alcohol excess. Never assume "not on blood thinners" excludes bleeding disorder.

Medication History: Name, dose, time of last dose of anticoagulant. INR if warfarin. Also aspirin, clopidogrel, DAPT, herbal supplements increasing bleeding risk.

Examination

General: Well, pale, anxious, haemodynamically unstable? Document obs before focusing on socket.

Intraoral: Good lighting + suction essential. First remove excess clot gently to identify source. Assess: active arterial bleeding, venous oozing, socket clot, torn gingiva, lacerations, loose sutures, retained roots, sharp bone. Do not assume extraction socket is only source.

Is It Really Bleeding?

Many describe: "My mouth is full of blood." Often saliva lightly stained with blood. Ask patient to rinse gently then observe socket directly. Simple step often prevents unnecessary intervention.

Investigations

Most require no investigations.

Bloods: If significant blood loss, medical comorbidity, admission likely. FBC, U&E, G&S if major bleeding, coagulation screen where clinically indicated.

INR: Only relevant for warfarin. Recent therapeutic INR does not exclude local causes but guides management.

Stepwise Management

Step 1 – Direct Pressure: Single most important intervention. Place folded damp gauze directly over socket. Patient bites firmly 20–30 minutes without checking. Every time clot disturbed, bleeding may restart.

Step 2 – Local Anaesthetic: If further intervention needed: LA with vasoconstrictor unless contraindicated. Benefits: pain relief, improved access, vasoconstriction, better cooperation.

Step 3 – Identify Bleeding Source: Carefully inspect: socket bleeding, soft tissue tears, bone bleeding, arterial bleeders, retained granulation tissue. Treat cause not just cover socket.

Step 4 – Local Haemostatic Measures: Depending on availability/local practice: resuture socket, absorbable haemostatic materials (oxidised regenerated cellulose, collagen, gelatin-based), firm socket packing, additional pressure. Choice depends on situation and protocols.

Step 5 – Reassess: Observe several minutes. If haemostasis achieved: instructions, follow-up if required. If bleeding persists: escalate to registrar.

Tranexamic Acid

TXA mouthwash may be used in selected patients per local protocols, particularly those at increased bleeding risk. Follow local guidelines.

Patients Taking Anticoagulants

Most extractions safely performed without stopping anticoagulants when protocols followed. When reviewing bleeding patient: Do NOT advise stopping anticoagulants without senior discussion. Instead: achieve local haemostasis, assess haemodynamic status, discuss persistent bleeding with reg. More on anticoagulants in separate teaching session.

When Should You Admit?

Consider if: bleeding cannot be controlled, repeated intervention required, significant blood loss, haemodynamically unstable, significant comorbidity, social circumstances unsafe, GA may be required for haemostasis.

Discharge Advice

Before discharge ensure patient understands: bite firmly on gauze if bleeding recurs, avoid vigorous rinsing first 24h after treatment, avoid smoking, avoid strenuous exercise remainder of day, sleep head elevated if possible, return immediately if bleeding heavy/continuous. Provide written instructions.

Red Flags ⚠️

Admit/urgent senior review: uncontrolled bleeding despite pressure + local measures, tachycardia/hypotension, >5 soaked gauzes, dizziness/syncope, known bleeding disorder, INR >4 on warfarin with active bleed, liver disease, posterior bleed not visualised.

Registrar Handover Prompt

Before calling: which tooth, timing, obs, active bleeding or oozing, anticoagulants, local measures tried, response to pressure, admission need. If asked “Why hasn’t this stopped?” think systematically: untreated local cause? Has effective pressure actually been applied? Systemic bleeding tendency? Anticoagulated?
