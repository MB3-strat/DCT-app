Facial Swelling

#facial-swelling#odontogenic-infection#airway#sepsis#trismus#on-call#red-flag#escalation
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

"Always assess the airway before the tooth."

Why This Matters

Facial swelling is the commonest emergency presentation seen by OMFS teams and one of the first conditions every Dental Core Trainee must learn to manage confidently. Although many patients have relatively localised odontogenic infections that can be treated safely with drainage and elimination of the source, a small proportion will develop rapidly progressive infections involving the deep fascial spaces of the head and neck. These infections can threaten the airway, spread into the mediastinum, result in overwhelming sepsis and, if untreated, become life-threatening. The challenge is not simply diagnosing an abscess — it is identifying the patient who is becoming critically unwell. Your priorities: assess the airway, recognise sepsis, identify the source, decide whether source control is required, escalate early.

Golden Rule

Always assess the airway before the tooth. Never become distracted by dental findings while overlooking a threatened airway.

The First Five Minutes – ABCDE

Airway: First priority. Look for stridor, difficulty speaking, difficulty swallowing saliva, drooling, “hot potato” voice, tongue elevation, floor of mouth swelling, respiratory distress. Ask: “Are you finding it difficult to breathe?” “Can you swallow your saliva?” If any concern: stay with patient, call registrar immediately, inform ED senior, involve anaesthetics early. Do NOT send unstable patient to CT before senior review.

Breathing: RR, SpO2, work of breathing, chest expansion, O2 requirement.

Circulation: HR, BP, CRT, perfusion. Look for sepsis.

Disability: Conscious level AVPU/GCS, confusion, glucose if appropriate.

Exposure: Extent of swelling, cellulitis, skin erythema, surgical scars, draining sinuses. Check temperature.

History

Presenting Complaint: Patient’s own words. “Left facial swelling for three days.”

HPC: Onset: sudden/gradual? Progression: improving/worsening/fluctuating? Rapid progression concerning. Pain: SOCRATES. Dental symptoms: toothache, broken tooth, recent extraction, previous swelling, previous antibiotics, RCT?

Red Flag Symptoms: Always ask specifically: difficulty swallowing, difficulty breathing, voice change, drooling, neck swelling, reduced mouth opening, fever, rigors.

Medical History: Diabetes, steroids, chemo, immunosuppression, previous H&N radiotherapy, bisphosphonates/denosumab, cardiac, pregnancy. Poorly controlled diabetes significantly increases risk.

Examination

General: Appearance, NEWS2, temperature, pulse, BP, RR, SpO2, hydration, distress.

Extraoral: Site, facial asymmetry, swelling, skin erythema, bruising, surgical scars. Palpate: tenderness, fluctuance, induration, crepitus, cervical nodes, mandibular border, salivary glands.

Mouth Opening: Always measure inter-incisal distance. Normal 35–50mm. <20mm significant trismus. Progressive trismus often indicates spread into muscles of mastication or adjacent spaces.

Intraoral: OH, carious teeth, pericoronitis, vestibular swelling, palatal swelling, floor of mouth, tongue position, pus discharge, tooth mobility, sinus tracts. Identify likely source.

Red Flags 🚩

Escalate immediately if: stridor, difficulty managing secretions, voice change, tongue elevation, raised floor of mouth, bilateral submandibular swelling, rapidly progressive swelling, trismus <20mm, sepsis, immunocompromised, failure to improve despite antibiotics, nursing/ED concern that patient deteriorating.

Investigations

Bloods: FBC, CRP, U&E, blood cultures if febrile/septic, VBG with lactate if systemically unwell, G&S if surgery anticipated. Remember: blood tests support assessment — they do not replace it.

OPG: Identify source tooth, impacted teeth, periapical pathology. Does not define extent of deep neck infection.

Contrast-Enhanced CT Neck: Investigation of choice when: deep space infection suspected, significant neck swelling, source uncertain, trismus/dysphagia/systemic toxicity, surgical drainage considered. Helps distinguish cellulitis from drainable abscess and defines extent.

Management Algorithm

ABCDE. Is airway threatened? Yes → Call registrar, anaesthetics, ED senior immediately. Prepare for definitive airway. No → Continue.

Establish source: Odontogenic, salivary, non-odontogenic?

Deep fascial space infection? Yes → Contrast CT if safe + discuss urgently with reg. No → Assess for localised infection.

Source control: Extraction of causative tooth where appropriate. I&D of drainable collection. Remember: antibiotics are adjunct, not substitute for source control.

Admission Criteria

Consider if: airway concerns, deep fascial space infection, significant trismus, dysphagia/odynophagia, requirement for IV antibiotics, requirement for surgical drainage, sepsis, significant comorbidities, failure of outpatient treatment, inadequate social support.

Indications for Surgery

Consider when: drainable abscess, failure of medical management, progressive infection, clear odontogenic source requiring removal, deep fascial space involvement. Objectives: eliminate source, drain pus, decompress infected spaces, obtain microbiology.

Antibiotics

Support definitive treatment. Choose per local antimicrobial guidelines, allergy status, severity, route. Never rely on antibiotics alone when source control required.

Discharge Criteria

Suitable if: no airway concerns, systemically well, localised infection, can tolerate oral intake, definitive treatment done or clear plan, follow-up arranged, understands safety-netting. Advise return if swelling progresses, swallowing difficult, or breathing changes.

SBAR: Situation — age, sex, location, problem. Background — PMH. Assessment — history, exam, obs, imaging. Recommendation — “I think... I'd like...”. Know: airway, NEWS2, mouth opening, source, floor of mouth, dysphagia, bloods, CT, working diagnosis, plan.
