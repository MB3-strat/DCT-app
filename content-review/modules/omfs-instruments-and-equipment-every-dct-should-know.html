OMFS Instruments and Equipment Every DCT Should Know

You do not need to know every instrument on day one—but you should recognise the common ones, understand their purpose and know how to use them safely. Anticipation in theatre begins with knowing the tray.

Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Module Matters

Every new OMFS DCT remembers standing in theatre hearing “Minnesota,” “Molt,” “Coupland,” “Langenbeck” with no idea what’s wanted. Consultants expect that on day one. Within weeks, you should recognise common instruments, understand their purpose, and anticipate the next step. This module isn’t an exhaustive catalogue — it’s the instruments you’ll use daily in dentoalveolar surgery, trauma, skin surgery, and facial procedures. Remember: Good assistants know the instruments. Great assistants know which one comes next.

Golden Rule

Never pass an instrument you cannot identify. If unsure, ask. Nobody expects a new DCT to know every instrument. Everyone expects you to learn.

Trauma instrumentation

1. Examination Instruments

Mouth Mirror: Examination, indirect vision, retraction.

College Tweezers: Handling gauze, small materials, sutures.

Periodontal Probe: Periodontal assessment, measuring defects, pockets.

2. Soft Tissue Instruments

Bard-Parker Scalpel Handle: Usually No.15 blade = workhorse for intraoral/skin incisions, biopsies. No.11 for stab incisions/drains.

Molt No.9 Periosteal Elevator: Most frequently used elevator in OMFS. Raises mucoperiosteal flaps, reflects periosteum, protects tissues. You’ll use this on almost every dentoalveolar list.

Freer Elevator: Delicate tissue elevation. Nasal surgery, septal work, facial trauma.

3. Retractors

Good surgery depends on exposure. Good exposure depends on good retraction.

Minnesota Retractor: Commonest in OMFS. Cheek retraction, flap protection, wisdom teeth. Assistant holds this most of the op.

Langenbeck Retractor: Deeper wounds, facial trauma, neck surgery, skin procedures.

Austin Retractor: Minor oral surgery, facial procedures.

Kilner / Cat’s Paw Retractor: Skin surgery, parotid surgery, delicate soft tissue handling.

4. Elevators

Coupland Elevators: Size 1, 2, 3. Luxating teeth, expanding sockets, root removal.

Warwick James Elevators: Straight, left, right. Excellent for root removal.

Cryer / East-West Elevators: Broken roots, sectioned molars, difficult extractions. Use carefully — high risk of fracture.

Root Picks: Fine instruments for small retained root tips. Avoid excessive force.

5. Extraction Forceps

Initially learn to recognise: Upper incisors, premolars, molars, upper roots. Lower universal, molars, lower roots. Don’t memorise numbers yet — recognition comes with experience.

6. Bone Instruments

Rongeurs / Bone Nibbler: Removing sharp bone, alveoloplasty, bone contouring.

Bone File: Used after rongeurs to smooth sharp bone.

Surgical Curette / Lucas Curette: Granulation tissue, periapical pathology, socket debridement.

Bone Chisel + Mallet: Less common now. Occasional osteotomy, trauma surgery.

7. Suction

Yankauer: Standard suction in OMFS. Rule: suction the blood — not the flap.

Frazier Suction: Fine suction for facial surgery, parotid surgery, delicate dissection.

8. Suturing Instruments

Mayo-Hegar Needle Holder: Standard needle holder. Learn correct grip, needle positioning, hand ties.

Adson Toothed Forceps: Ideal for skin closure — teeth reduce tissue crush.

Non-Toothed Forceps: Delicate tissues, mucosa, dressings.

Suture Scissors: Know where they are. You’ll use constantly.

9. Powered Equipment

Surgical Handpiece: Bone removal, tooth sectioning, osteotomy. Always ensure irrigation working + soft tissues protected.

Straight Handpiece: Wisdom teeth, apicectomy, minor oral surgery.

Sagittal Saw: Trauma, orthognathic. Not commonly handled by DCT but recognise.

Oscillating Saw: Bone grafting, craniofacial, orthognathic.

10. Trauma Instruments

Recognise: drill guides, drill bits, depth gauge, plate benders, screwdrivers, reduction forceps, titanium plates, screws. Initially just understand purpose.

Safe Instrument Passing

Always: pass firmly, present in position it will be used, pass sharps handle first, warn surgeon when passing blade. Never throw instruments onto tray.

Looking After Instruments

Never: drop instruments, use for unintended purposes, damage delicate tips, place heavy instruments on microsurgical ones. Treat with respect — expensive and precision engineered.

Theatre Etiquette

Before every case: introduce yourself to scrub, learn team names, check patient positioning, confirm imaging displayed, wear eye protection. The scrub team teaches you as much as consultants.

DCT Checklist: Before You Assist in Theatre

✓Procedure planned
✓Instruments required
✓Correct handpiece + irrigation available
✓Correct burs
✓Plates/screws if trauma
✓Appropriate sutures
✓Imaging displayed

Best assistants watch the operation — not just the tray

A good assistant provides exposure, keeps field clear, anticipates, handles tissues gently, communicates

Registrar Handover Prompt

If asked to prepare: “Procedure is bilateral mandible ORIF. I’ve checked: drill + handpiece + irrigation, 2.0 plates + screws, reduction forceps, Langenbeck retractors, Minnesota, Molt, Yankauer, imaging displayed. Sutures 3-0 Vicryl + 5-0 Prolene ready.”

DCT Survival Box 🚨

Learn These Ten Instruments First

Before month 1 in OMFS, confidently identify:

✓No.3 Bard-Parker scalpel handle
✓Molt No.9 periosteal elevator
✓Minnesota retractor
✓Langenbeck retractor
✓Coupland elevators
✓Warwick James elevators
✓Lucas curette
✓Mayo-Hegar needle holder
✓Adson toothed forceps
✓Yankauer suction

If you recognise these ten without hesitation, you’re ready for most dentoalveolar and minor oral surgery procedures.

Clinical Pearl – The Consultant's Question

If asked: "What makes a good theatre assistant?"

Strong answer: "A good assistant provides excellent exposure, keeps the operative field clear with effective suction, anticipates the next step, handles tissues gently and communicates clearly with the surgeon and scrub team."

Technical knowledge matters, but anticipation, situational awareness and teamwork distinguish an excellent assistant
