The First 100 Days – Becoming a Safe and Confident OMFS DCT

Competence in OMFS is not achieved in a week or a month. It develops through curiosity, reflection, repetition and the humility to ask for help. Your goal is not to know everything—it is to become a doctor your patients and colleagues can trust.

#professionalism#on-call#competence#escalation#reflection#teamwork#confidence#DCT#non-technical-skills
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Module Matters

Technical skills get you through procedures. Non-technical skills get you through the job. The first 100 days of OMFS are steep, disorienting, and exhausting. You’ll feel slow, uncertain, and hyper-aware of what you don’t know. That’s normal. This module is about surviving and thriving: how to build trust, recognise your limits, learn from each case, and become the DCT that nurses bleep first because you’re safe. Patients remember how you made them feel. Consultants remember whether you were reliable. Your aim isn’t perfection — it’s progression.

Golden Rule

Be safe before you are fast. Be kind before you are clever. Be honest before you are right.

The Non-Negotiables – Do These Every Day

Show up 10min early. Check your bleeps, review the list, look at inpatients before handover.

Read the operation note on every post-op patient you review. Most questions are answered there.

Write your name + bleep + time on every entry. Own your decisions.

Reassess after every intervention. If you gave fluids, recheck BP. If you reduced a jaw, recheck occlusion.

Debrief yourself. After every shift ask: What went well? What would I do differently? What do I need to learn?

Competence ≠ Knowing Everything Competence =

Recognising sick vs not sick within 30 seconds.

Starting ABCDE without being told.

Knowing your hard limits and calling before you cross them.

Communicating so others aren’t surprised by your patient’s condition at 3am.

Escalation: The Skill That Saves Careers

Call early if: NEWS2 ↑, airway concern, active bleeding, you’ve done 2 interventions with no improvement, you’re thinking “should I call?” — that’s your answer.

How to call: Use SBAR. Be specific about what you need: “I need you to come and see” vs “FYI”. Never apologise for calling. Apologise if you don’t call and harm occurs.

Building Trust – The Currency of OMFS

With Nurses: Answer bleeps. Say thank you. If they’re worried, you’re worried. Close the loop: “Thanks for calling, I’ve reviewed, plan is X”.

With ED: See referrals promptly. Give a decision. Don’t sit on patients. Write clear plans.

With Registrars: Be prepared before you call. Know obs, PMH, what you’ve done. Take feedback without ego.

With Consultants: Be honest. Be prepared for theatre. Know your patients. Follow up tasks.

With Patients: Introduce yourself. Explain in plain English. Admit when you don’t know. They forgive ignorance, not arrogance.

Reflection – Your Superpower After a difficult case, ask:

What happened? Facts only.

So what? Why did it matter? What did I feel?

Now what? What will I change next time?

Write it down. One paragraph per week. You’ll be shocked how fast you improve.

Looking After Yourself

Sleep: Protect post-nights. No one is safe on 2h sleep.

Food: Carry snacks. Hypoglycaemia makes you irritable and stupid.

Support: Find your people — other DCTs, regs, mentors. Talk.

Boundaries: You can’t fix the whole hospital. Do your best, handover, go home.

Mistakes: You will make them. Disclose, reflect, learn. That’s how consultants were made.

DCT Checklist: Weekly Review

✓One new clinical skill learned?
✓One piece of feedback requested + acted on?
✓One patient I’m proud of how I managed?
✓One system issue I flagged to improve care?
✓Did I eat, sleep, exercise at least 3x this week?

Be the DCT you’d want looking after your family — that’s the standard

DCT Survival Box 🚨

The “Am I Safe?” Test at 2am

Before you make a decision, ask:

If I had to defend this in coroner’s court, could I?

Have I told someone more senior?

If any answer is no → stop, rethink, escalate.
