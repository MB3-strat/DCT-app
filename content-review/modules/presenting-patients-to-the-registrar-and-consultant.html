Presenting Patients to the Registrar and Consultant

A clear presentation is a clinical skill. It demonstrates organisation, prioritisation and sound clinical reasoning.

#communication#escalation#on-call#patient-safety#SBAR#professionalism#referrals
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

Every DCT will present patients to a registrar or consultant multiple times during every on-call shift. A concise, structured presentation enables rapid decision-making, improves patient safety and demonstrates professionalism. Your aim is not simply to relay information—you should interpret it, formulate a working diagnosis and propose an initial management plan. Senior clinicians do not expect perfection. They expect you to have assessed the patient, recognised the important findings and communicated them clearly.

Golden Rule

Never phone your registrar until you know what you are asking. Before making the call, ask yourself: Am I asking for advice? Am I requesting a review? Do I think the patient needs admission? Do I think the patient needs theatre?

Core Content

Before You Pick Up the Phone

Ensure you know: patient name, hospital number, age, location, relevant medical history, observations/NEWS2, presenting complaint, key examination findings, blood results, imaging findings, working diagnosis, initial management, your recommendation. If you cannot answer these, review the patient again.

The One-Minute Presentation

Identify the Patient: "Hello, it's Dr Jones, the OMFS DCT. I'm calling about a 42-year-old gentleman in ED Majors."

Presenting Problem: "He has a two-day history of worsening left facial swelling associated with LL7." One sentence.

Relevant Background: Only include information that changes management: diabetes, anticoagulation, immunosuppression, previous radiotherapy, significant allergies.

Your Assessment: General condition, observations, airway, key exam findings. "NEWS2 is 2. Temperature 38.3°C. Airway is patent with no stridor or voice change. Left submandibular swelling, trismus 18mm, grossly carious LL7."

Investigations: Summarise important findings. "CRP is 165, WCC 17.4. CT demonstrates left submandibular collection without significant airway compression."

Your Impression: "I think this is a left submandibular space infection of odontogenic origin."

Your Plan: Finish with recommendation. "I'd like to admit him, commence IV antibiotics and discuss operative drainage."

The Consultant Wants to Know

Every senior will mentally ask: Is the patient sick? Is the airway safe? What is the diagnosis? Does the patient need admission? Does the patient need surgery? What have you already done? Answer these six questions.

Red Flags ⚠️

Escalate immediately if: airway compromise, significant haemorrhage, sepsis, deep neck infection, visual loss/diplopia after trauma, orbital compartment syndrome, rapidly deteriorating patient, any uncertainty regarding management.

DCT Checklist: Before Calling

✓Patient age and location
✓Mechanism or presenting complaint
✓Observations and NEWS2
✓Airway status
✓Examination findings
✓Imaging findings
✓Blood results
✓Working diagnosis
✓Your proposed plan

Registrars are assessing your clinical reasoning, not your memory

A concise one-minute presentation is more effective than a five-minute monologue

Key Takeaways

Structure every presentation the same way — situation, background, assessment, recommendation

Lead with the diagnosis and clinical status — not the chronology

Always include observations and airway assessment — seniors need to know if the patient is stable

Clear communication improves safety and builds confidence as an OMFS clinician

Registrar Handover Prompt

Use SBAR. Situation: patient details + problem. Background: relevant PMH. Assessment: history, exam, obs, imaging. Recommendation: "I think..." "I'd like...". Be ready to answer: What worries you most? What do you want me to do?
