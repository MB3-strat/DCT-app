Mandibular Fractures

Treat the patient first, the fracture second. Airway, occlusion and associated injuries always take priority over the radiograph.

Why This Matters

Mandibular fractures are among the commonest facial fractures managed by OMFS. Frequently encountered following assaults, falls, sporting injuries and RTCs. Most are not immediately life-threatening but may be associated with airway compromise, significant haemorrhage, cervical spine injury, intracranial injury, or other facial fractures. Your role is not simply to identify a fracture on imaging — it is to assess the entire patient, recognise associated injuries and communicate findings effectively to seniors. A patient with a normal CT but an unrecognised airway problem is far more dangerous than a patient with a mandibular fracture waiting for surgery.

Golden Rule

The most important examination finding in a mandibular fracture is the patient's occlusion. If the bite is normal, significant displacement is less likely. If the bite has changed, assume a fracture until proven otherwise.

Relevant Anatomy

The mandible forms a ring-like structure. A force strong enough to fracture the mandible often produces a second fracture elsewhere. Always actively search for additional fractures. Common sites: condyle, angle, body, parasymphysis, symphysis, ramus, coronoid process.

Mechanisms of Injury

Assault: Most common cause in many OMFS units. Frequently causes angle fractures and parasymphysis fractures.

Falls: More common in children/elderly. Frequently associated with condylar injuries.

Sporting Injuries: Often isolated fractures.

RTC: High-energy. Often associated with multiple fractures, head injury, C-spine injury.

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Airway: Patency, facial swelling, oral bleeding, loose teeth, foreign bodies. Look for posterior displacement of fractured segments, bilateral parasymphyseal fractures causing tongue displacement, expanding floor of mouth haematoma. Airway compromise uncommon but potentially life-threatening.

Breathing: RR, SpO2, chest injuries.

Circulation: Pulse, BP, active bleeding. Most mandibular fractures bleed modestly, but significant oral bleeding can occur.

Disability: GCS, pupils, head injury symptoms. Ask: LOC, vomiting, amnesia.

Exposure: Other injuries, facial lacerations, limb injuries, neck injuries.

History

Mechanism: How did injury occur? Punch to jaw ≠ bicycle accident.

Timing: When did injury happen?

Symptoms: Pain, malocclusion, trismus, numbness, difficulty chewing, difficulty speaking. Patient's description of altered occlusion is highly reliable.

Examination

General Inspection: Facial asymmetry, swelling, bruising, lacerations. Look from front and above.

Occlusion: Most important part. Ask: "Do your teeth meet together normally?" Assess directly. Look for premature contact, open bite, midline shift, crossbite, new malocclusion. Document carefully.

Palpation: Symphysis, parasymphysis, body, angle, ramus, condyles. Assess tenderness, step deformity, crepitus, mobility.

Mouth Opening: Measure and document. Reduced opening may indicate pain, muscle spasm, condylar injury, coronoid injury.

Intraoral: Gingival tears, haematoma, step deformity, tooth fractures, mobile segments. Sublingual haematoma = classic sign of mandibular fracture.

Inferior Alveolar Nerve: Assess lower lip/chin sensation. Document carefully. Mental nerve paraesthesia common with body/angle fractures.

Common Fracture Patterns

Open vs Closed Fractures

Mandibular fracture considered open if it communicates with: oral cavity, skin laceration, PDL space of tooth-bearing segment. Most dentate mandibular fractures are effectively open fractures. Implications for antibiotics and definitive management.

Red Flags 🚩

Urgent senior review if: airway compromise, bilateral mandibular fractures, significant displacement, open fracture with contamination, associated facial fractures, neurological deficit, polytrauma, uncontrolled haemorrhage.

Investigations

OPG: Useful for initial assessment, fracture location, dentition assessment. Limitations: less useful for condyles, may underestimate complex injuries.

CT Facial Bones: Investigation of choice. Provides fracture location, degree of displacement, additional facial fractures, surgical planning information. Now standard in most trauma centres.

Initial Management

Analgesia: Provide appropriate pain relief early.

Soft Diet: Advise soft diet, avoid chewing hard foods.

Oral Hygiene: Recommend good OH, antiseptic mouth rinses where appropriate.

Antibiotics: Follow local protocols. Many units prescribe for open mandibular fractures, particularly with oral cavity communication. Practice varies.

Definitive Management

Depends on: fracture location, degree of displacement, occlusion, dentition, patient factors.

Conservative: Selected minimally displaced fractures with stable occlusion.

Closed Reduction: Arch bars, IMF.

ORIF: Titanium plates/screws. Indications: displacement, malocclusion, unstable fractures, multiple fractures.

Admission Criteria

Consider if: airway concerns, polytrauma, significant pain, inability to maintain oral intake, requirement for surgery, social concerns. Many admitted pending operative treatment.

Discharge Criteria

Selected patients discharged if: airway stable, pain controlled, oral intake adequate, clear follow-up arranged, definitive treatment planning occurred. Provide clear advice re worsening pain, swelling or occlusion changes.

Key Takeaways

Assess patient using trauma principles before focusing on fracture — ABCDE first

Occlusion is the most important clinical finding in mandibular trauma

Always look for a second mandibular fracture — ring structure means forces often cause two

CT is imaging of choice, but management guided by patient not just scan

Registrar Handover Prompt

Structure: Mechanism, airway status, LOC, occlusion, mouth opening, IAN status, open/closed, CT findings, associated injuries, plan. If you can’t answer “What is the patient’s occlusion like?” you have not completed the examination. Consultants agree: mandibular fracture assessment is incomplete without occlusion documented.
