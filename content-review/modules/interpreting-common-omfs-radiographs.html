Interpreting Common OMFS Radiographs

Never look at an X-ray simply to confirm what you think you know. Look at it systematically, because the most important finding is often the one you were not expecting.

#radiology#OPT#CBCT#CT-facial-bones#CT-neck#USS#fractures#impacted-teeth#IAC#incidental-findings
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Module Matters

Radiographs are integral to OMFS. As a DCT you’ll review imaging in ED, clinic, ward rounds and theatre. The skill is systematic interpretation — not spot diagnosis. Radiographs complement, but never replace, history and examination. This module covers common OMFS modalities and gives you a structured approach so you don’t miss the second mandibular fracture, the antral carcinoma, or the IAC running through a molar root.

Golden Rule

Every radiograph deserves a systematic review—even if the diagnosis seems obvious. Never look only at the area of interest. Always review the entire image.

Core Content

BEFORE YOU REVIEW ANY RADIOGRAPH

Ask:

✓Is this the correct patient?
✓Is the image correctly labelled?
✓What imaging modality is this?
✓Why was the image taken?
✓What clinical question am I trying to answer?

Common Imaging Modalities in OMFS

Become familiar with: Periapical PA, Bitewing, DPT/OPT, Occlusal, CBCT, CT facial bones, CT neck with contrast, Ultrasound, MRI.

A Systematic Approach – Every Time

Step 1 – Confirm Patient Details

Name, DOB, NHS/hospital number, date of imaging. Wrong patient = wrong treatment.

Step 2 – Image Quality

Is it diagnostic? Movement? Anatomy adequately visualised? Repeat required? Poor films mislead.

Step 3 – Review the Entire Image

Never zoom into pathology first. Look at: bones, teeth, sinuses, soft tissues where visible, airway, TMJs, cervical spine if included. Many incidental findings discovered this way.

Dental Panoramic Tomography (OPT/DPT)

The commonest OMFS image. Assess systematically:

Teeth: Missing, impacted, caries, root morphology, periapical pathology, retained roots.

Bone: Cortical continuity, fractures, bone loss, cysts, tumours, osteomyelitis.

Inferior Alveolar Canal: Always identify position, corticated borders, relationship to lower 8s. Look for: interruption of superior border, darkening of roots, diversion, narrowing. Critical before lower third molar surgery.

Maxillary Sinuses: Opacification, fluid levels, mucosal thickening, foreign bodies, bony destruction.

TMJs: Condylar shape, cortical outline, symmetry, degenerative change.

Periapical Radiographs

Ideal for: individual teeth, root morphology, PDL space, periapical lesions, endodontics. Look at: lamina dura, root apex, furcation, crestal bone.

Bitewing Radiographs

Best for: proximal caries, crestal bone levels, existing restorations. Do NOT use to diagnose apical pathology.

Occlusal Radiographs

Useful for: salivary stones, impacted teeth, foreign bodies, floor of mouth pathology, cortical expansion.

Cone Beam CT (CBCT)

High-resolution 3D of dentoalveolar skeleton. Indications: impacted teeth, roots vs IAN, implant planning, jaw pathology, endodontics, selected trauma. Excellent for bone, limited soft tissue. Review entire volume, not just region of interest.

CT Facial Bones

Investigation of choice for most facial fractures. Assess systematically: mandible, maxilla, zygomas, orbits, nasal bones, frontal sinus, skull base. Review axial, coronal, sagittal. 3D recons for visualisation only — never replace axial/coronal/sagittal review.

CT Neck with Contrast

For: deep neck space infection, salivary abscess, neck collections, airway compromise, malignancy. Look for: rim-enhancing collections, gas, fascial plane involvement, airway deviation/narrowing, vascular complications.

Ultrasound

Indications: salivary gland disease, neck lumps, abscesses, lymph nodes. Advantages: no radiation, dynamic, guided aspiration.

MRI

Best for: soft tissue tumours, salivary lesions, TMJ disc, perineural spread, osteomyelitis. Superior soft tissue contrast vs CT.

Common Radiographic Findings

Incidental Findings

Never ignore: impacted teeth, carotid calcification, retained roots, bone lesions, elongated styloid, cervical spine degeneration. Document significant findings and discuss appropriately.

Red Flags 🚨

Escalate immediately: displaced mandible fractures, midface instability, orbital floor defect with entrapment, airway narrowing on CT neck, rim-enhancing collection with gas, IAC running through molar roots with signs of compression, unexpected malignancy features: bony destruction, periosteal reaction.

Presenting Radiographic Findings

Structured example: “This is a panoramic radiograph of the correct patient taken today. There is a mesioangular impacted LL8 with intimate radiographic relationship to the IAC demonstrated by interruption of the superior cortical border and darkening of the roots. No associated cystic change seen. Remaining dentition and jaws show no other significant abnormality.”

DCT Checklist: Before Discussing Imaging with Registrar

✓Imaging modality + date
✓Main abnormality + side
✓Relationship to important structures — IAC, maxillary sinus, orbital floor
✓Evidence of infection? Fracture?
✓Soft tissue involvement?
✓Incidental findings?
✓Your interpretation

When uncertain, ask for senior review rather than guessing

Clinical Pearl – The Consultant's Question

If asked: "What is the commonest mistake junior doctors make when interpreting radiographs?"

Good answer: "They search for the expected abnormality instead of reviewing the entire image systematically. This leads to satisfaction-of-search error, where identifying one lesion causes additional important findings to be overlooked."

Developing a consistent search pattern is one of the most valuable habits in OMFS.

DCT Survival Box 🚨

The "IMAGE" Rule

Whenever you review an OMFS radiograph, remember IMAGE:

I – Identity: Is this the correct patient?

M – Modality: What type of imaging is this?

A – Anatomy: Review the entire image systematically.

G – Generate a differential: Correlate radiographic findings with clinical picture.

E – Escalate if uncertain: Never be afraid to seek senior advice.

Common Mistakes ⚠️

Looking only at tooth of interest, missing second mandibular fracture, ignoring maxillary sinuses on OPT, overcalling normal anatomy as pathology, forgetting to identify IAC before lower 8s, failing to review whole CBCT volume.
