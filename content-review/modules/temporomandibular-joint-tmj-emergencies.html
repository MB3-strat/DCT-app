Temporomandibular Joint (TMJ) Emergencies

Not every patient who cannot close their mouth has a dislocated jaw—but every true TMJ dislocation should be recognised promptly, reduced safely and investigated for the underlying cause.

#TMJ-dislocation#trismus#condyle#mandibular#reduction#recurrent-dislocation#septic-arthritis#on-call#red-flag
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Module Matters

TMJ emergencies are relatively uncommon, but every OMFS DCT will encounter them on-call. Most are acute anterior TMJ dislocations, but referrals include condylar fractures, acute trismus, septic arthritis of the TMJ and post-op complications. Distinguishing these is essential because management differs significantly. Most acute TMJ dislocations can be reduced safely in ED with analgesia, muscle relaxation and good technique. Repeated unsuccessful attempts increase muscle spasm, making reduction harder. Your priorities: confirm diagnosis, exclude associated fractures, provide analgesia, reduce safely where appropriate, recognise when senior help/theatre required.

Golden Rule

A patient with a true bilateral TMJ dislocation cannot close their mouth—don't keep asking them to try. Once diagnosed, focus on reduction rather than repeated examination.

Relevant Anatomy

TMJ = mandibular condyle, glenoid fossa, articular eminence, articular disc, joint capsule, muscles of mastication. Most acute dislocations are anterior — condyle trapped anterior to articular eminence. Posterior, superior, lateral dislocations rare and associated with significant trauma.

Common Presentations

Mouth locked open, inability to speak clearly, drooling, jaw pain, anxiety, difficulty swallowing saliva, preauricular discomfort. Diagnosis usually clinical.

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Most isolated TMJ dislocations don’t compromise airway. Always ABCDE if: facial trauma, reduced GCS, polytrauma, significant bleeding. Airway can be threatened if bilateral condylar fractures with posterior displacement.

History

Mechanism: Yawning, laughing, dental treatment, vomiting, GA, seizures, facial trauma. Trauma raises suspicion for condylar fractures.

Previous Episodes: "Has this happened before?" Recurrent dislocations common in: generalised ligamentous laxity, connective tissue disorders, chronic TMJ instability.

PMH: Parkinson’s, epilepsy, connective tissue disorders, previous TMJ surgery.

Drugs: Antipsychotics → acute dystonic reaction mimics dislocation.

Examination

Inspection: Mouth persistently open, inability to occlude teeth, excess salivation, distress. Patients often support mandible with both hands.

Palpation: Preauricular region. Condyles palpable anterior to articular eminence in dislocation. Empty glenoid fossa.

Occlusion: In true anterior bilateral dislocation, patient unable to achieve occlusion. This distinguishes dislocation from many fractures.

Cranial nerves: Assess V3 sensation — inferior alveolar nerve. CN VII if trauma present.

Differential Diagnosis

History usually provides diagnosis.

Red Flags 🚩

Escalate if: trauma mechanism, suspected fracture, first presentation with uncertain diagnosis, failed reduction, persistent pain after reduction, facial nerve weakness, inability to rule out condylar fracture, systemic illness.

Investigations Imaging:

No imaging needed: Obvious atraumatic recurrent anterior dislocation, successful reduction, no trauma.

Imaging required: Trauma, suspicion of fracture, first presentation uncertain, failed reduction, persistent pain.

CT Facial Bones: Preferred if fracture suspected. Shows condylar position + fractures.

OPG: May show condylar position but less useful than CT in trauma. Can miss fractures.

Initial Management

Provide: analgesia — NSAIDs + paracetamol, +/- opioids. Reassurance. Quiet environment. Muscle spasm ↑ with anxiety and repeated attempts.

Manual Reduction – Principles

Must be attempted by appropriately trained clinicians.

Principle: Mandible must be depressed inferiorly to disengage condyle from articular eminence before guiding posteriorly into glenoid fossa. Movement is downwards first, then backwards. Pushing back alone fails.

Practical Tips:

Sit patient upright or slightly reclined. Support occiput against wall/chair.

Explain procedure. Get patient to relax — jaw will “drop” when muscles relax.

Adequate analgesia + muscle relaxation: consider Entonox, IV midazolam 1–2mg, or procedural sedation per local policy.

Operator: gloves, gauze over thumbs. Thumbs on mandibular molars/occlusal surface. Fingers grip inferior border mandible extraorally.

Apply firm steady pressure downwards to overcome masseter spasm, then guide posteriorly.

Be prepared to withdraw thumbs quickly — mandible snaps shut.

Bilateral: reduce one side at a time or simultaneously depending on preference.

Alternative Techniques: Conventional intraoral, wrist pivot, syringe technique for selected uncomplicated anterior dislocations, extraoral techniques. Follow local practice/consultant preference.

After Successful Reduction

Reassess: occlusion — should return to pre-morbid, mouth opening, pain, mandibular movements. Document reduction clearly: “Bilateral TMJ dislocation reduced successfully. Occlusion restored. Patient comfortable.”

OMFS follow-up if recurrent or first time

Some patients benefit from supportive bandage/Barraquer wire splint immediately post-reduction per local protocol

When Reduction Fails

Causes: muscle spasm, delayed presentation >12–24h, fracture, incorrect diagnosis, patient unable to relax. Do NOT continue repeated forceful attempts. Escalate to reg. Reduction under sedation/GA may be required. Consider LA injection to joint or muscle relaxant. If still fails → theatre.

Recurrent Dislocation

Refer for specialist assessment. Options: physiotherapy, occlusal splints selected patients, botulinum toxin to lateral pterygoid selected cases, eminectomy, Dautrey procedure, capsular plication in refractory cases.

Septic Arthritis of the TMJ

Uncommon but serious. Suspect if: fever, severe preauricular pain, trismus, swelling, raised inflammatory markers, systemically unwell. Needs urgent imaging — USS or contrast CT, IV antibiotics, senior review. May need joint aspiration/washout in theatre.

Consultant Pearls 💡

Most atraumatic anterior dislocations are clinical diagnoses — don’t CT everyone

Document occlusion before and after — medicolegal and functional importance

If patient has had GA recently, think bilateral condylar fractures not dislocation

Reassess and document occlusion after reduction — confirms success

Registrar Handover Prompt

“23F, atraumatic bilateral TMJ dislocation after yawning. Third episode this year. No trauma, occlusion deranged, cannot close mouth. No imaging. One attempt reduction in ED unsuccessful due to spasm. Plan: Entonox + IV midazolam, second attempt. If fails, for theatre. Follow-up OMFS clinic re recurrent dislocation.”

Fracture version: “67M fall, hit chin. Mouth open, anterior open bite, can partially close. Tender bilateral preauricular. CT: bilateral condylar fractures. Not TMJ dislocation. For admission, soft diet, OMFS review.”

Do I know the correct reduction manoeuvre? Down then back

If this fails, when will I stop and call for help? 2 attempts max before senior

Safe practice is recognising your limits as well as your skills.
