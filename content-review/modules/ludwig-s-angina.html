Ludwig's Angina

#airway#emergency#sepsis#odontogenic-infection#deep-neck-space#escalation#red-flag
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

"Ludwig's angina is an airway emergency until proven otherwise."

Why This Matters

Ludwig's angina is one of the few true life-threatening emergencies in OMFS. It is a rapidly spreading cellulitis involving bilateral submandibular, sublingual and submental spaces. Oedema, rather than pus, causes early airway compromise as the tongue is displaced superiorly and posteriorly. Mortality was historically >50%. Modern management has improved outcomes, but delay kills. Your priorities: protect the airway, recognise sepsis, start treatment, escalate early, achieve source control.

Golden Rule

If you think it might be Ludwig's angina, treat it as an airway emergency until proven otherwise. Never be falsely reassured by a patient who is still talking.

Core Content

What Is Ludwig's Angina?

Rapidly progressive cellulitis, usually odontogenic from mandibular molars. Involves submandibular, sublingual, submental spaces bilaterally. Early problem is diffuse inflammatory oedema causing airway compression.

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

A - Airway: Stridor, voice change “hot potato”, drooling, inability to swallow saliva, tongue elevation, floor of mouth swelling, respiratory distress. Ask: “Can you swallow your saliva?” If no, escalate immediately. Do NOT repeatedly examine mouth if airway compromised.

B - Breathing: RR, SpO2, accessory muscle use. O2 if indicated.

C - Circulation: HR, BP, CRT, temp. Look for sepsis.

D - Disability: GCS, confusion, glucose.

E - Exposure: Bilateral submandibular swelling, diffuse neck swelling, warmth, woody induration. Neck often firm, not fluctuant.

Red Flags ⚠️

Immediate senior review if: stridor, drooling, difficulty swallowing saliva, voice change, tongue elevation, bilateral submandibular swelling, raised floor of mouth, rapid deterioration, respiratory distress, sepsis, reduced GCS. One red flag justifies escalation.

Initial Management

ABCDE. Call registrar, consultant, anaesthetics, senior ED clinician. Early multidisciplinary involvement saves lives.

Move to resus area. Continuous monitoring. Do not leave unattended.

IV access x2. Bloods: FBC, CRP, U&E, blood cultures, VBG with lactate, G&S.

IV fluids if indicated.

IV antibiotics per local guidelines — broad spectrum with anaerobic cover.

Assess need for definitive airway. Options: awake fibre-optic intubation, video-assisted intubation, surgical airway. Decision by anaesthetics + OMFS senior. Never attempt advanced airway beyond competence.

Do not delay airway management for CT. Unstable patient belongs in resus/theatre.

Investigations

Contrast-Enhanced CT Neck: Defines extent, abscess vs cellulitis, airway deviation, mediastinal spread. But do not delay airway management to obtain it.

Definitive Surgical Management

Once airway secure: remove source tooth, incise and drain involved spaces if indicated, obtain microbiology, irrigate, place drains. Not every patient has mature abscess — decompression may still be required.

Definitive management: airway protection, antibiotics, source control

Registrar Handover Prompt

SBAR: “32M in ED with Ludwig’s angina. Stridor, unable to swallow saliva, tongue elevated, NEWS 7. I’m concerned about airway. Anaesthetics en route. Need you now. Shall we prepare for theatre?”
