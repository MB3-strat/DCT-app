Midface Fractures

The eye, the occlusion and the airway matter more than the fracture pattern. Always assess function before classification.

Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

Midface fractures range from isolated nasal fractures to complex panfacial injuries involving maxilla, zygoma, orbit and skull base. For new DCTs, it’s easy to become distracted by CT classifications. The first priorities are simpler: Is the airway safe? Can the patient see normally? Is there an ocular emergency? Is the occlusion altered? Is there a CSF leak? Only after these are answered should you describe fracture patterns. You are treating an injured patient—not a CT scan.

Golden Rule

Every patient with a midface fracture deserves an eye examination. Failure to examine and document vision is one of the commonest and most serious errors in facial trauma.

Initial Assessment – ATLS Principles

Airway: Blood, loose teeth, facial swelling, posterior displacement of fractures, vomiting. High-energy facial injuries may rapidly compromise airway.

Breathing: RR, SpO2, associated chest injuries.

Circulation: Pulse, BP, external bleeding. Control facial bleeding with direct pressure.

Disability: GCS, pupils, head injury symptoms. Ask: LOC, vomiting, amnesia.

Exposure: Facial asymmetry, bruising, lacerations, other traumatic injuries.

History

Mechanism: Punch, fall, RTC, sport. Mechanism predicts pattern.

Time of injury.

Visual symptoms: Loss of vision, double vision, blurred vision, floaters.

Facial numbness: Cheek, upper lip, side of nose.

Difficulty biting: Altered occlusion.

Epistaxis: Unilateral/bilateral, persistent.

Previous facial fractures.

PMH: Anticoagulants, bleeding disorders, relevant ophthalmic history — glaucoma, previous surgery, monocular vision.

The Eye Examination

This is mandatory. Document every component. Do it before swelling makes assessment impossible.

Red Flags 🚩

Urgent senior + ophthalmology review if: reduced visual acuity, RAPD, suspected globe rupture, retrobulbar haemorrhage, progressive proptosis, severe restriction of eye movements, entrapment especially in children, hyphaema. These are true emergencies.

Retrobulbar Haemorrhage

Orbital compartment syndrome. Signs: severe pain, proptosis, tense orbit, reduced VA, RAPD, ophthalmoplegia. Requires emergency lateral canthotomy/cantholysis. Time critical — minutes to irreversible visual loss.

Facial Examination

Palpation: Frontal bone, supraorbital rims, infraorbital rims, zygomatic arches, nasal bones, maxilla. Look for step deformities, tenderness, crepitus, mobility.

Occlusion: Ask patient to bite together. Assess premature contacts, open bite, crossbite, mobility of maxilla. Malocclusion suggests significant skeletal injury. Mobile maxilla is never normal.

V2 Sensation: Lower eyelid, cheek, side of nose, upper lip. Reduced sensation common in ZMC and orbital floor fractures.

Common Midface Fractures

Septal Haematoma

Blood collection between septal cartilage and mucoperichondrium. Exam: bilateral boggy swelling of septum, bluish discolouration. Must drain urgently — untreated causes septal necrosis and saddle-nose deformity. Use nasal speculum + headlight.

CSF Rhinorrhoea

Clear fluid from nose. Ask: salty taste, worse leaning forward. Test: halo sign, glucose positive, beta-2 transferrin. Indicates skull base fracture. Needs neurosurgery discussion.

Investigations

CT Facial Bones: Imaging of choice. Shows fracture pattern, displacement, orbital involvement, associated facial fractures. Review yourself before calling reg.

Additional Imaging: CT Head, CT C-spine, CT Angiography if vascular injury suspected.

OH advice — gentle, chlorhexidine if tolerated

Antibiotics: Follow local protocols for open fractures. Not routine for all.

Advise: Do NOT blow nose if orbital fracture suspected — risk of orbital emphysema and visual compromise. Avoid straining, heavy lifting.

When Should You Admit?

Airway compromise, significant ocular injury, retrobulbar haemorrhage, CSF leak, multiple facial fractures, severe pain requiring inpatient management, associated head injury, requirement for urgent surgery.

Discharge Criteria

Vision normal, no ocular emergency, pain controlled, oral intake adequate, follow-up arranged, understand return precautions. Safety-net: immediate return if worsening vision, increasing pain, new diplopia, persistent clear nasal discharge, increasing swelling, difficulty breathing.

Key Takeaways

Every midface fracture patient needs documented eye exam — VA, pupils, EOM, diplopia

Visual acuity recorded before anything else in ophthalmic assessment

Assess occlusion in every facial trauma patient — malocclusion = significant injury

Never miss septal haematoma or retrobulbar haemorrhage — both cause permanent damage if delayed

Treat function first — vision, airway, occlusion matter more than memorising classifications

Registrar Handover Prompt

Lead with function: “30M, assault. No airway concern. VA 6/6 R, 6/9 L. No RAPD. Restricted upgaze L with diplopia. V2 paraesthesia L. Occlusion stable. CT: L orbital floor + ZMC #. Needs ophthalmology review for entrapment. For admission and probable theatre.”

Avoid: “He’s got a Le Fort II.” Start with what worries you: “Airway safe. Worried about vision — VA reduced and RAPD present.”
