Facial Skin and Soft Tissue Abscesses

Where there is pus, it usually needs drainage. Antibiotics alone rarely cure a mature abscess. Your job is to recognise when a facial infection has become a surgical problem.

Why This Module Matters

Not every facial swelling is dental in origin. OMFS teams frequently assess facial skin and soft tissue infections: infected epidermoid (sebaceous) cysts, infected traumatic wounds, post-op wound infections, infected haematomas, cellulitis, spontaneous facial abscesses. Most are straightforward, but some are rapidly progressive infections requiring urgent surgery, while others may be infected malignancies or deep fascial space infections masquerading as superficial swellings. Your role: differentiate cellulitis from abscess, identify patients suitable for LA drainage, recognise when admission/theatre required, exclude underlying pathology, escalate early when infection spreading or patient systemically unwell.

Golden Rule

Drain the pus—but always ask why it formed. A facial abscess is often secondary to an underlying problem that also requires treatment.

Common Causes

Facial soft tissue abscesses commonly arise from: infected epidermoid (sebaceous) cysts, infected traumatic wounds, post-operative wound infections, infected haematomas, folliculitis/furunculosis, infected skin lesions, insect bites, foreign bodies, salivary gland infections (covered separately). Not every fluctuant swelling is "just a cyst."

Cellulitis vs Abscess

History

Ask: duration, rapid progression, previous episodes, recent trauma, previous surgery, skin lesions, insect bites, diabetes, immunosuppression, MRSA history, fever/rigors. Recurrent abscesses → consider underlying cyst, foreign body, or immunocompromised state.

Examination

Inspect: Site, size, skin colour, necrosis, pointing, previous scars, sinus formation.

Palpate: Fluctuance, induration, tenderness, crepitus, regional lymphadenopathy.

Always assess: Surrounding tissues. A small abscess may be surrounded by extensive cellulitis.

Facial nerve: Document CN VII function before any intervention. Weakness changes management urgently.

Red Flags 🚩

Urgent senior review if: rapid progression, significant facial swelling affecting eye, facial nerve weakness, skin necrosis, crepitus, severe systemic illness, failure to improve on antibiotics, immunocompromise, suspicion of necrotising soft tissue infection.

Necrotising Soft Tissue Infection

Rare but must consider. Features: pain out of proportion, rapid progression, skin discolouration, bullae, crepitus, systemic toxicity. Surgical emergency. Escalate immediately — do not wait for imaging or abx response.

Investigations

Bloods: FBC, CRP, U&E, blood cultures if septic.

Ultrasound: Useful for distinguishing cellulitis from abscess, identifying superficial collections, guiding aspiration.

CT with Contrast: If infection extensive, deep extension suspected, orbital involvement, uncertain diagnosis, recurrent infection, failure of treatment.

Incision and Drainage – Principles

Indications: Fluctuance, pointing, imaging-confirmed collection, failure of antibiotics.

Incision planning: Follow relaxed skin tension lines where possible. Large enough for adequate drainage. Avoid important aesthetic subunits where possible. Respect facial nerve branches — lies immediately deep to SMAS in many areas.

Technique: LA, prep, incise, send pus for MC&S where appropriate, break down loculations with artery forceps, irrigate copiously, consider small drain or ribbon gauze.

Key point: A tiny incision into a large abscess rarely works.

Antibiotics

Indicated for: associated cellulitis, systemic features, immunocompromised, extensive surrounding inflammation. Routine antibiotics NOT always necessary after complete drainage of small uncomplicated abscess in well patient. Follow local antimicrobial guidance.

When Should You Worry?

Consider alternative diagnoses if: swelling hard rather than fluctuant, recurrent infections at same site, delayed healing, lesion ulcerated, unexplained tissue loss, history of skin cancer. An infected skin malignancy can mimic abscess. Do not excise suspected malignancy without senior input.

Consultant Pearls 💡

Most recurrent facial abscesses arise from underlying epidermoid cyst — excise formally once infection settled

Avoid excising infected cyst during acute phase unless consultant advises — inflamed planes make complete excision difficult, recurrence likely

Always consider underlying cause — cyst, foreign body, malignancy, haematoma

Do not attempt formal cyst excision during acute infection — I&D first, excise later

Respect facial nerve anatomy — document function pre-op, plan incision carefully

Never dismiss recurrent/atypical lesions — infected SCC/BCC presents as “abscess”

Clinical Pearl – The Consultant's Question

If asked: "Why does this patient keep getting the same abscess?"

Good answer: "The infection is likely secondary to an underlying epidermoid cyst or retained foreign body. Drainage treats the acute infection, but definitive management requires addressing the underlying pathology once inflammation has resolved." Treating abscess alone without removing cause leads to recurrence.
