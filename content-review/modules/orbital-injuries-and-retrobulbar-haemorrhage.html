Orbital Injuries and Retrobulbar Haemorrhage

The patient who loses vision from an untreated retrobulbar haemorrhage has suffered a preventable complication. Recognise it early, act decisively and call for help immediately.

#orbital-trauma#retrobulbar-haemorrhage#orbital-compartment-syndrome#vision-loss#emergency#ophthalmology#lateral-canthotomy#red-flag#on-call
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

Orbital injuries are common following facial trauma, but retrobulbar haemorrhage (RBH) is rare. Despite its rarity, every OMFS DCT must recognise it because it is one of the few true sight-threatening emergencies in maxillofacial surgery. Bleeding behind the globe rapidly increases intraorbital pressure, producing orbital compartment syndrome. As pressure rises, optic nerve and retinal perfusion fall. If not relieved promptly, irreversible visual loss may occur within hours. The diagnosis is clinical. Do not wait for a CT scan if orbital compartment syndrome is strongly suspected. Your priorities: recognise the condition, assess vision immediately, escalate to reg + ophthalmology without delay, prepare for emergency lateral canthotomy + inferior cantholysis if indicated. Never allow imaging to delay sight-saving treatment.

Golden Rule

Vision first. CT later. If orbital compartment syndrome is suspected clinically, decompression should not be delayed while awaiting imaging.

What Is a Retrobulbar Haemorrhage?

Bleeding into orbital space behind the globe. Orbit is a confined bony compartment — even small volume of blood causes marked rise in intraorbital pressure → compression of optic nerve, compression of retinal circulation, orbital compartment syndrome, permanent blindness.

Causes

Facial trauma, orbital fractures, orbital surgery, peribulbar/retrobulbar anaesthetic injections, endoscopic sinus surgery, anticoagulation, spontaneous bleeding (rare).

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Orbital injuries frequently occur with multiple injuries. Always complete trauma assessment first.

Airway: Facial swelling, bleeding, associated fractures.

Breathing: Respiratory status.

Circulation: Control major bleeding, document obs.

Disability: GCS, head injury symptoms.

Exposure: Inspect entire face. Orbital injuries frequently occur alongside ZMC, Le Fort, nasal fractures.

History

Ask: exact mechanism, time of onset of visual symptoms, has vision deteriorated, double vision, eye pain, pain on eye movement, previous eye disease, contact lens use, anticoagulants. Sudden deterioration after initially normal vision is particularly concerning.

The Eye Examination – <2 Minutes

Visual Acuity: Most important finding. Test each eye separately. Use Snellen chart, reading text, counting fingers, hand movements, perception of light. Never document "Vision appears normal".

Pupils: Equality, direct/consensual responses, RAPD. An RAPD strongly suggests optic nerve dysfunction.

Eye Movements: All directions. Restricted upward gaze may suggest muscle entrapment, orbital swelling, or compartment syndrome.

Diplopia: Ask: "Do you see one image or two?" Determine: constant?, direction of gaze?

Visual Fields: Confrontation testing if possible.

Globe Position: Proptosis, enophthalmos, vertical dystopia. Rapidly progressive proptosis highly concerning.

Eyelids: Tense swollen eyelids, ecchymosis, lacerations. Rock-hard swollen eyelid should immediately raise suspicion of orbital compartment syndrome.

IOP: If available and no suspicion of globe rupture, IOP measurement supports diagnosis. Do NOT delay treatment to obtain this.

Investigations

CT Facial Bones: Demonstrates orbital fractures, RBH, globe displacement, muscle entrapment. However: normal CT performed later does not reverse permanent visual loss. If diagnosis clinically obvious, treatment takes priority over imaging.

Emergency Management

Step 1: Call immediately — your registrar, ophthalmology, anaesthetic support if required.

Step 2: Assess and document: vision, pupils, RAPD, eye movements. Repeat frequently.

Step 3: Elevate head to ∼30°. Avoid unnecessary pressure on eye.

Step 4: If orbital compartment syndrome strongly suspected and senior help not immediately available: prepare for emergency lateral canthotomy + inferior cantholysis. This is a sight-saving procedure. Every OMFS trainee must understand why, when, and anatomy. Even if not yet performing independently, recognise when it cannot wait.

Lateral Canthotomy and Inferior Cantholysis

Indications: Suspected orbital compartment syndrome with visual deterioration, RAPD, proptosis, tense orbit, raised orbital pressure.

Principle: Releases lateral canthal tendon, allowing globe to move forward, reducing orbital pressure. Does not treat haemorrhage. Buys time to preserve optic nerve perfusion.

Note: This is covered in practical skills sessions. Do not attempt without senior guidance unless life/limb/sight threat and no senior immediately available.

Medical Therapy

Adjuncts may be considered following senior discussion per local protocols: mannitol, acetazolamide, high-dose steroids. These do NOT replace surgical decompression where orbital compartment syndrome is present.

Globe Rupture

Always consider. Features: irregular pupil, low IOP, hyphaema, uveal prolapse, obvious penetrating injury, positive Seidel test. If suspected: do NOT apply pressure, shield the eye, urgent ophthalmology review.

Admission

Most patients with suspected RBH require admission for monitoring and ophthalmology input.

Key Takeaways

Retrobulbar haemorrhage causing orbital compartment syndrome is a true sight-threatening emergency

Visual acuity and pupil exam should be performed immediately and documented clearly — these are your baselines

An RAPD, deteriorating vision and tense proptosed eye trigger immediate senior escalation

Do not delay treatment while waiting for CT if orbital compartment syndrome strongly suspected

Lateral canthotomy + inferior cantholysis are sight-saving — every OMFS trainee must understand indications

Registrar Handover Prompt

Lead with vision: “Assault 1 hour ago. RE visual acuity counting fingers only, was 6/6 earlier. RAPD present. Proptosed, tense orbit, pain on movement. Suspected orbital compartment syndrome. Ophthalmology called. Requesting immediate review for lateral canthotomy.” Fracture pattern is secondary to functional deficit.

Clinical Pearl – The Consultant's Question

If asked: "What finding would make you most worried?"

The answer is not: "A fractured orbit."

It is: "A patient with deteriorating vision, RAPD, progressive proptosis and a tense orbit. Those findings indicate orbital compartment syndrome until proven otherwise and require immediate decompression." The fracture can wait. The optic nerve cannot.
