Avulsed Permanent Teeth

Every minute a permanent tooth remains dry reduces the chance of successful replantation. Time is the most important treatment.

#avulsion#dental-trauma#replantation#periodontal-ligament#splinting#emergency#paediatrics#red-flag
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

Avulsion is the complete displacement of a tooth from its socket and is one of the few true dental emergencies. No other dentoalveolar injury is so critically dependent on time. Prognosis depends largely on survival of periodontal ligament (PDL) cells attached to the root surface. These cells begin to lose viability as soon as the tooth dries out. Immediate replantation offers the greatest chance of long-term survival, while prolonged dry storage significantly increases risks of inflammatory root resorption and ankylosis. Your priorities: confirm this is a permanent tooth, minimise extra-oral dry time, replant ASAP where appropriate, stabilise, arrange follow-up. The clock starts the moment the tooth leaves the socket—not when the patient arrives.

Golden Rule

Never scrub the root. Protect the periodontal ligament. The root surface contains viable PDL cells essential for healing.

The First Question: Is It a Permanent Tooth?

This is the most important question.

Permanent Tooth: Usually replant if appropriate.

Primary Tooth: Never replant. Risks damage to developing permanent successor. This is one of the highest-yield facts in exams and on-call.

The Biology of Avulsion

Long-term prognosis depends on: PDL cell survival, root surface integrity, extra-oral dry time, storage medium, stage of root development. Pulpal survival is secondary to preservation of PDL.

Time Matters

Prognosis worsens as extra-oral dry time increases.

<15 minutes: Excellent prognosis. Immediate replantation ideal.

<60 minutes: Good chance of periodontal healing if stored appropriately.

>60 minutes dry: PDL survival poor. Replantation still usually indicated for permanent teeth, but counsel re high risk of ankylosis/replacement resorption. Delayed replantation may still preserve alveolar bone and provide functional/aesthetic benefits, particularly in younger patients.

Patient’s saliva – buccal sulcus if conscious/cooperative

Normal saline

Avoid plain water — damages PDL cells due to low osmolarity.

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Airway: Ensure no fragments threaten airway. If tooth missing, always consider aspiration, ingestion, intrusion.

Breathing: Assess for thoracic injury if significant trauma.

Circulation: Control facial bleeding. Assess other injuries.

Disability: GCS, head injury symptoms, LOC.

Exposure: Facial lacerations, alveolar fractures, other dental injuries.

History

Ask: Exact time of injury, mechanism, where tooth kept, was it cleaned, any replantation attempt, LOC, vomiting, other facial injuries.

PMH: Immunocompromise, bleeding disorders, tetanus status.

Examination

Extraoral: Facial injuries, lip lacerations, swelling, bruising. Always palpate for facial fractures.

Intraoral: Empty socket, socket integrity, alveolar fractures, gingival lacerations, occlusion, other damaged teeth. Inspect avulsed tooth. Do not scrape root. Handle by crown only.

Immediate Management

Step 1: Confirm tooth is permanent.

Step 2: Rinse visible debris gently with sterile saline if required. Do NOT scrub, curette, or remove attached PDL tissue.

Step 3: LA if required. Inspect socket. Remove only obvious clot preventing replantation. Do NOT aggressively curette socket.

Step 4: Replant tooth gently with firm digital pressure. Ensure correct orientation. Tooth should seat fully.

Step 5: Confirm position clinically. Assess occlusion.

Step 6: Apply passive flexible splint. Current IADT guidance: ∼2 weeks for uncomplicated avulsion. Longer if associated alveolar fracture.

Step 7: Arrange follow-up with appropriate dental trauma service.

Antibiotics

Systemic antibiotics generally recommended following replantation of avulsed permanent tooth, particularly where contamination occurred. Choice follows local protocols and current IADT guidelines, considering age, PMH, allergies, pregnancy.

Tetanus

Assess tetanus status. Contaminated injuries may require vaccination/booster per national guidance.

Radiographs

Usually: periapical, occlusal, OPG if multiple injuries. Assess: tooth position, root fracture, alveolar fracture, intrusion of adjacent teeth.

Red Flags ⚠️

Do NOT replant if: primary tooth, patient medically unfit, extensive alveolar fracture preventing stability, tooth grossly contaminated and cannot be cleaned without damaging PDL, significant medical contraindication. Discuss with senior.

Urgent senior review if: cannot achieve replantation, associated facial fractures, airway concern, aspiration suspected, significant head injury.

Complications

Discuss possibility of: pulp necrosis, inflammatory root resorption, replacement resorption, ankylosis, tooth loss. May develop months/years later despite optimal treatment.

Follow-Up

Long-term review required. Typical follow-up: clinical exam, pulp sensibility testing, radiographs at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, then yearly. May continue several years.

Admission

Rarely required unless: associated facial fractures, significant head injury, GA required, multiple injuries, safeguarding concerns.

DCT Survival Box 🚨 The Three Rules of Avulsion

Permanent teeth are usually replanted.

Primary teeth are never replanted.

Never hold the tooth by the root.

If you remember these three rules, you will avoid the commonest mistakes.
