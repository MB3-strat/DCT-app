Dry Socket (Alveolar Osteitis)

A dry socket is intensely painful but almost never dangerous. Your role is to recognise it, exclude more serious pathology and provide effective pain relief and reassurance.

#dry-socket#alveolar-osteitis#post-extraction#pain#complications#dental-emergency
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

Dry socket is one of the commonest complications after dental extraction and a frequent reason for emergency attendance. Patients are often extremely distressed because pain can be severe, radiate widely, and appear disproportionate to clinical findings. The commonest mistake is prescribing antibiotics. Dry socket is not primarily an infection and antibiotics have little/no role in uncomplicated cases. Your role: confirm diagnosis, exclude other pathology, provide pain relief, reassure, promote healing.

Golden Rule

Severe pain with very little swelling is usually dry socket until proven otherwise.

Core Content

What Is Dry Socket?

Alveolar osteitis occurs when normal blood clot in extraction socket is lost or breaks down prematurely. Without clot: alveolar bone exposed, nerve endings uncovered, healing delayed, severe pain develops. Importantly: no established suppurative infection in uncomplicated dry socket. This explains why antibiotics are generally ineffective.

Incidence

1–5% routine extractions. Up to 30% mandibular third molar surgical extractions. Highest after difficult lower 8s.

Risk Factors

Surgical extraction, mandibular thirds, smoking, previous dry socket, oral contraceptive use, difficult/traumatic extraction, poor OH, pre-existing infection, increasing age. Smoking is strongest modifiable risk factor.

Pathophysiology

Premature fibrinolysis destroys clot → socket empty → bone exposed → healing delayed → pain fibres directly stimulated. Pain often far greater than clinical appearance.

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Not an emergency, but systematic assessment still required. Most have normal airway, breathing, circulation. Disability: pain may be severe despite normal exam. Exposure: look for facial swelling, cellulitis, bruising. Systemic abnormality should prompt reconsideration.

History

Timing: Most useful clue. Pain develops 2–5 days post-extraction. Immediate post-op pain less likely dry socket.

Pain: Severe, deep, constant, throbbing, radiating to ear/temple/neck, poorly relieved by simple analgesics.

Associated: Bad taste, halitosis, difficulty eating, facial swelling, fever, trismus. Swelling/fever uncommon in uncomplicated dry socket.

Examination

Extraoral: Usually normal. No significant swelling, cellulitis, neck swelling. Document obs.

Intraoral: Empty socket, partial clot loss, exposed bone, grey/yellow fibrin, food debris, local tenderness. Surrounding gingiva relatively healthy. Gentle irrigation improves visibility.

Typical Clinical Features

Classic triad: severe pain + empty socket + delayed onset 2–5 days post-extraction. Highly suggestive of alveolar osteitis.

Red Flags ⚠️

Reconsider diagnosis if: facial swelling, fever, trismus, pus discharge, progressive swelling, difficulty swallowing, failure to improve after local management, severe pain beyond 2 weeks. Suggests alternative diagnosis or secondary infection.

Investigations

Most require no investigations.

Radiographs: Consider PA or OPG if suspect retained root, tooth fragment, fractured bone, foreign material, alternative pathology. Routine imaging unnecessary in straightforward cases.

Management

Step 1 – Gentle Irrigation: Warm saline to remove food debris. Avoid vigorous curettage — may delay healing.

Step 2 – Pain Relief: Appropriate analgesia per patient needs and PMH. Advise regular analgesia.

Step 3 – Socket Dressing: Depending on local practice, obtundant dressing may be placed for temporary pain relief. Reduces exposed bone sensitivity. May need review/replacement. Clinicians should know products used locally.

Step 4 – Patient Advice: Explain: painful but self-limiting, healing 7–14 days, pain should gradually improve, gentle rinsing after 24h, smoking delays healing. Reassurance is therapeutic.

Antibiotics

Routine antibiotics NOT indicated. Consider only if: cellulitis, spreading infection, systemic illness, immunocompromise with concern for secondary infection. Prescribing for uncomplicated dry socket exposes patients to adverse effects without benefit.

Follow-Up

Review if: pain remains severe, repeat dressing needed, symptoms worsen, diagnosis uncertain. Most improve substantially within days.

Admission

Almost never required. Consider only if: significant spreading infection develops, severe medical comorbidity complicates management, alternative pathology requires inpatient care.

Key Takeaways

Dry socket presents 2–5 days post-extraction with severe pain, minimal swelling, empty socket

If swelling, trismus, fever or pus present, think beyond dry socket

Registrar Handover Prompt

Before calling: which tooth, date of extraction, timing of pain onset, swelling present/absent, socket appearance, exposed bone, pus
