Le Fort Fractures

Le Fort fractures are not simply patterns on a CT scan—they represent instability of the midface. Always assess airway, occlusion and maxillary mobility before worrying about the classification.

Why This Module Matters

Le Fort fractures are high-energy injuries involving separation of the maxilla from the facial skeleton. Most commonly seen following RTCs, significant falls, industrial accidents and major assaults. Although classically divided into Le Fort I, II and III, most modern injuries are combinations rather than “pure” patterns. For the OMFS DCT, recognising a Le Fort fracture is less important than recognising what it means: the patient has sustained major facial trauma, the airway may deteriorate rapidly, there may be associated cranial, cervical and ocular injuries, occlusion is frequently disrupted, and definitive treatment usually requires operative fixation. Your role: identify the injury, recognise associated life-threatening problems, ensure appropriate escalation.

Golden Rule

If the upper jaw moves independently from the rest of the face, think Le Fort fracture. Maxillary mobility is an important clinical finding but should only be assessed gently and once, as repeated manipulation is painful and may worsen bleeding.

Understanding Le Fort Fractures

René Le Fort described predictable fracture patterns following blunt facial trauma. Useful for communication, but remember: real trauma rarely follows textbook lines. Patients often have mixed injury patterns.

Initial Assessment – ATLS Principles

Airway: Priority. Compromise may result from posterior displacement of maxilla, bleeding, loose teeth, vomiting, soft tissue swelling. Manage per trauma protocols.

Breathing: RR, SpO2, chest injuries.

Circulation: Pulse, BP, active facial bleeding. Control haemorrhage with direct pressure where appropriate.

Disability: GCS, pupils, head injury symptoms. Always consider traumatic brain injury.

Exposure: Inspect entire patient. Le Fort fractures rarely occur in isolation.

History

Mechanism: High-energy implied. RTC, fall from height, assault with weapon.

LOC/Vomiting/Amnesia: Suggests head injury.

Visual symptoms: Blurred vision, double vision.

Altered bite: New malocclusion.

Loose upper teeth: Maxillary mobility.

Facial numbness: V2 distribution.

Clear nasal discharge: Unilateral watery rhinorrhoea post-trauma raises concern for CSF leak.

Examination

Inspection: Facial swelling, bilateral periorbital bruising (raccoon eyes), epistaxis, nasal deformity, midface flattening, facial asymmetry, lacerations.

Eye Examination: Mandatory. Document VA each eye, pupils + RAPD, eye movements, diplopia, globe position. Do not omit.

Occlusion: Ask patient to bite together. Look for anterior open bite, posterior premature contacts, crossbite, new malocclusion. Document carefully.

Maxillary Mobility: Only after excluding significant C-spine concerns. Gloved hands: stabilise forehead, gently grasp upper incisors, assess abnormal movement. Do this ONCE. Repeated examination unnecessary and harmful.

Cranial Nerves: V2 — reduced sensation cheek, upper lip, side of nose. Facial nerve if soft tissue injuries.

Associated Injuries: Always look for orbital, ZMC, nasal, mandibular, skull base, C-spine fractures. Finding one facial fracture should prompt search for others.

Red Flags 🚩

Urgent senior/anaesthetics/neurosurgery/ophthalmology review if: airway threatened, visual loss, RAPD, globe rupture, retrobulbar haemorrhage, CSF rhinorrhoea, uncontrolled epistaxis, GCS drop, significant displacement, bilateral mandibular fractures, polytrauma.

CSF Leak

Suspect if: clear unilateral rhinorrhoea, persistent watery nasal discharge, salty taste, associated skull base fracture. Test: halo sign, glucose positive, beta-2 transferrin. Management: avoid nasal instrumentation, no NG tubes via nose until senior review, nurse 30° head up, discuss immediately with reg/neurosurgery. Prophylactic antibiotics controversial — follow local protocol.

Investigations

CT Facial Bones: Investigation of choice. Assess fracture pattern, maxillary displacement, orbital involvement, skull base extension, associated facial fractures. Review images yourself before discussion.

Additional Imaging: CT Head, CT C-spine, CT Angiography if vascular injury suspected. Trauma series as per ATLS.

Initial Management

Airway protection: Definitive airway if compromised. Maxillofacial injuries may require surgical airway.

Analgesia: Adequate early.

Haemorrhage control: Direct pressure, anterior/posterior nasal packs if required, consider interventional radiology for refractory epistaxis.

Soft diet/NBM: If surgery anticipated, keep NBM.

Careful oral hygiene: Chlorhexidine mouthwash if able.

Antibiotics: Most Le Fort fractures communicate with oral cavity → open fractures. Antibiotic use varies — follow local OMFS protocols.

Definitive Management

Most displaced Le Fort fractures require: open reduction, internal fixation using titanium plates, restoration of occlusion, reconstruction of facial buttresses. Precise surgical approach depends on fracture configuration. Managed via MDT with maxillofacial, neurosurgery, ophthalmology, ICU.

Admission

Nearly all true Le Fort fractures require admission. Reasons: high-energy mechanism, airway observation, operative planning, associated injuries, requirement for multidisciplinary management.

Key Takeaways

Le Fort fractures are high-energy injuries — always perform full trauma assessment first

Document vision and ocular findings in every patient — VA, pupils, EOM, diplopia

Assess occlusion and maxillary mobility carefully — single gentle examination

Look actively for associated facial, cranial and cervical injuries — they rarely occur alone

Most displaced Le Fort fractures require admission and operative management — airway + fixation

Registrar Handover Prompt

Lead with function not classification: “High-energy RTC. Airway patent, C-spine cleared. VA 6/6 bilaterally, no RAPD, no diplopia. Anterior open bite, maxilla mobile on gentle testing. V2 paraesthesia bilaterally. CT: Le Fort II pattern with orbital floor involvement, no skull base fracture seen. No CSF leak. Plan: admit, NBM, IV abx per protocol, theatre for ORIF, neurosurgery aware.”

Avoid: “He’s got a Le Fort II.” Focus on what matters: airway, vision, occlusion, stability, associated injuries.
