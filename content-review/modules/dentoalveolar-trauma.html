Dentoalveolar Trauma

Time is tissue. The earlier dentoalveolar injuries are recognised and managed appropriately, the better the long-term outcome for both teeth and supporting structures.

Why This Matters

Dentoalveolar injuries account for a significant proportion of emergency OMFS referrals, particularly in children, adolescents and young adults. They commonly result from falls, sporting injuries, assaults, road traffic collisions and recreational accidents. Unlike many other OMFS emergencies, the success of treatment is often determined by how quickly and how accurately the injury is managed. Delayed diagnosis, inappropriate splinting or failure to reposition displaced teeth can result in pulpal necrosis, ankylosis, root resorption and ultimately tooth loss. Your role: exclude life-threatening injuries, perform systematic dental/facial assessment, identify injury type, reposition and stabilise teeth where indicated, arrange follow-up. Remember: the patient is more important than the tooth.

Golden Rule

Treat the patient before treating the tooth. A fractured incisor should never distract you from a cervical spine injury or significant facial trauma.

Initial Trauma Assessment – ATLS Principles

Airway: Patency, facial bleeding, loose teeth, avulsed teeth, foreign bodies. Remove loose fragments only if airway risk.

Breathing: RR, SpO2, chest injuries.

Circulation: BP, pulse, active bleeding. Control facial haemorrhage with direct pressure.

Disability: GCS, pupils, neuro status. Ask: LOC, vomiting, amnesia.

Exposure: Facial lacerations, bruising, swelling, other injuries. Never assume dental trauma occurs in isolation.

History

Mechanism: Fall, assault, sport, bicycle, RTC — predicts injury pattern.

Timing: Exactly when did injury occur? Critical for avulsed teeth.

LOC: Black out, vomiting, confusion? Head injury may take precedence.

Missing Teeth: "Do you know where every tooth is?" If not, consider aspiration, ingestion, intrusion. Missing tooth never simply assumed lost at scene.

Medical History: Anticoagulants, bleeding disorders, tetanus status for contaminated injuries.

Examination

Extraoral: Facial asymmetry, swelling, lacerations, bruising. Palpate: orbital rims, zygomas, nasal bones, mandible. Assess mandibular movements.

Cranial Nerves: Document infraorbital nerve, mental nerve, facial nerve where indicated.

Intraoral: Missing teeth, tooth mobility, displacement, fractures, gingival lacerations, occlusion, bite changes. Always compare with opposite side.

Classification of Dental Injuries

Alveolar Fractures

Assess for: segment mobility, occlusal changes, gingival lacerations. Teeth often move as one unit. Treatment: repositioning, flexible splinting, soft diet, OMFS follow-up.

Inability to achieve normal occlusion after repositioning

Investigations

Radiographs: Periapical, OPG, occlusal views depending on injury.

CT Imaging: If facial fractures suspected, multiple injuries, high-energy trauma, significant alveolar fractures.

CXR: If tooth missing and aspiration cannot be excluded.

Soft Tissue Injuries

Always inspect lips carefully. Lacerations frequently contain tooth fragments/enamel/foreign material. Failure to remove embedded fragments causes delayed complications. Radiograph lip if fragment unaccounted for.

Splinting Principles

Current evidence supports flexible splinting for most dentoalveolar injuries. Rigid splints avoided unless specifically indicated, e.g. certain alveolar fractures. Duration depends on injury type — follow current international guidance (IADT).

Antibiotics

Routine antibiotics NOT indicated for every dental injury. Consider in: avulsed teeth, contaminated wounds, associated soft tissue injuries. Follow local protocols and current trauma guidelines.

Tetanus

Consider prophylaxis for: contaminated injuries, soil contamination, animal-related injuries. Follow national guidance.

Admission Criteria

Uncommon but consider if: associated facial fractures, significant soft tissue injuries, airway concerns, multiple injuries, GA required, safeguarding concerns, significant head injury.

Discharge Advice

Advise: soft diet 1–2 weeks, excellent OH, chlorhexidine mouthwash if appropriate, avoid contact sports, attend all follow-up, return if increasing mobility/swelling/pain.

Key Takeaways

Complete ATLS assessment before concentrating on dentition — life-threatening injuries first

Always account for every tooth — missing teeth may be aspirated, swallowed or intruded

Flexible splinting + appropriate follow-up are key to long-term outcomes

Registrar Handover Prompt

Know: mechanism, time of injury, head injury symptoms, teeth involved, missing teeth accounted for, type of injury, alveolar fracture present?, occlusion, radiographs, splinting need. Structure: “Fall 2 hours ago, no LOC, UL1 avulsed, accounted for, replanted at 20min, LA + flexible splint applied, tetanus given, OPG shows no root fracture. Plan review 2 weeks.”
