Your First On-call Shift

The purpose of your first on-call is not to manage every patient independently. Your job is to assess patients safely, recognise the unwell patient, initiate appropriate management and escalate appropriately.

Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Matters

For many DCTs, the first on-call shift is the most daunting part of starting OMFS. The bleep may seem relentless, referrals can arrive simultaneously from multiple departments, and many patients will have medical as well as dental problems. Remember that you are never expected to work in isolation. The registrar and consultant are there to support you, and early escalation is a sign of good judgement—not weakness.

Golden Rule

Never let the bleep dictate your priorities. The loudest referral is not necessarily the sickest patient. Always ask: Who needs me first?

Core Content

Before Your Shift Starts

Arrive 15–20 minutes early. Ensure you know: registrar's name/contact, consultant on call, how to contact anaesthetic team, ED/theatres/wards/radiology locations, PACS/EPR access, local antibiotic guidelines, emergency theatre pathway, CEPOD booking. Carry: hospital bleep, charged phone, ID badge, pen, notebook.

Prioritising Referrals

Receiving a Telephone Referral

Record: referrer name/designation, patient name, hospital number, age, location, responsible consultant. Ask: "What is your specific concern?" Clarify exactly why OMFS input is required. Get: current observations, NEWS2, airway status. Never assume the patient is stable.

Your First Assessment

Step 1: ABCDE assessment

Step 2: Focused history

Step 3: Targeted examination

Step 4: Review imaging — never rely solely on report

Step 5: Formulate working diagnosis, differentials, immediate plan

Step 6: Decide: admit, discharge, discuss, operate?

Any uncertainty — if you are worried, call

DCT Checklist: Before Finishing Every Referral

✓Have I assessed the airway?
✓Have I recorded the observations?
✓Have I documented the history and examination?
✓Have I reviewed any available imaging?
✓Have I started appropriate initial management?
✓Have I discussed with my senior if indicated?
✓Have I documented the outcome of that discussion?
✓Does the patient need review again?

End of Shift Checklist

Before handing over: ensure all outstanding referrals reviewed/handed over, document pending investigations, highlight unstable patients, communicate anticipated problems, confirm responsibility for follow-up actions.

Consultant Pearls 💡

The patient who appears "too well" may still deteriorate quickly—repeat assessments are often more informative than a single examination

Read the CT yourself before discussing it with your registrar

If you have not examined the patient, avoid making management decisions over the telephone

Prepare before speaking to your registrar by gathering all relevant information

Clear documentation and effective handover are as important as clinical decision-making

Registrar Handover Prompt

Before calling: patient age, relevant medical history, mechanism/complaint, observations, airway status, examination findings, imaging findings, blood results, working diagnosis, proposed plan. A concise presentation allows timely decisions.
