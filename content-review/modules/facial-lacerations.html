Facial Lacerations

A well-repaired facial laceration may be almost invisible in a year's time. A poorly repaired one may be remembered for life. Good technique begins with good assessment.

Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Module Matters

Facial lacerations account for a significant proportion of OMFS emergency referrals. Although many appear straightforward, they often coexist with facial fractures, dental injuries, nerve injuries, salivary duct injuries or ocular trauma. The cosmetic and functional consequences of poor management can last a lifetime. Repairing facial lacerations is one of the first practical skills you will develop as an OMFS DCT. Success depends far less on suturing technique than on your ability to assess the wound properly, handle tissues gently and restore normal anatomy. Remember: The operation starts the moment you examine the wound—not when you pick up the needle holder.

Golden Rule

Never close a wound until you know what lies beneath it. Every facial laceration should be considered a potential injury to deeper structures until proven otherwise.

Initial Assessment

Always perform ABCDE before concentrating on the wound. Large facial lacerations can be distracting. Do not miss: airway compromise, C-spine injury, head injury, major haemorrhage, ocular injury.

History

Mechanism: Fall, assault, glass, knife, animal bite, RTC, sport. Mechanism predicts contamination and underlying injury.

Timing: Exact time of injury, any previous attempts at closure.

Symptoms: Facial numbness, weakness, double vision, difficulty chewing, difficulty closing eye, altered bite. These suggest injuries beyond skin.

Examination

Step 1 – Control Bleeding: Direct pressure, LA with adrenaline where appropriate, haemostatic measures if required. Avoid immediately placing sutures into bleeding wound. Good haemostasis improves visibility enormously.

Step 2 – Clean the Wound: Before deciding closure: remove dirt, gravel, glass, tooth fragments, clothing fibres. Copious irrigation with normal saline is one of the most important parts of wound management. The solution to pollution is dilution.

Step 3 – Examine Properly: Assess length, depth, tissue loss, contamination, devitalised tissue, foreign bodies. Good lighting essential.

Look for Underlying Injuries

Never assume a laceration is "just a cut." Specifically assess for: facial fractures, dental injuries, parotid duct injury, facial nerve injury, globe injury, lacrimal system injury, cartilage exposure, muscle injury.

Facial Nerve Examination

Any wound over parotid or cheek prompts facial nerve exam. Ask patient to: raise eyebrows, close eyes tightly, smile, puff out cheeks, show teeth. Document findings BEFORE local anaesthetic if possible.

Parotid Duct Injury

Suspect if wound crosses line joining tragus to midpoint of upper lip. Signs: saliva leaking into wound, difficulty expressing saliva into mouth. These injuries require senior review. Consider probing or sialography per local protocol.

Specialised Structures

Choosing Local Anaesthetic

Common: Lidocaine 1% with adrenaline, Lidocaine 2%, Bupivacaine for prolonged analgesia. Adrenaline provides better haemostasis, visibility, longer anaesthesia. Avoid intravascular injection. Always aspirate. Avoid adrenaline in end arteries — fingers, penis — but face is safe.

Suture Selection

Follow local consultant preference.

Principles of Wound Closure

Based on: gentle tissue handling, anatomical alignment, layered closure, minimal tension, accurate skin edge approximation, slight wound edge eversion. Do not strangulate tissues with overly tight knots.

Layered Closure

Close from deep to superficial: 1. Muscle 2. Deep dermis 3. Skin. Eliminate dead space where possible.

Dog Ears

Minor standing cones often flatten with time. Do not aggressively excise normal tissue to achieve immediate perfection.

Tissue Loss

If tissue loss prevents tension-free closure: do NOT force wound together. Discuss with registrar. Skin grafts or local flaps may be required.

Red Flags 🚩

Escalate immediately: facial nerve weakness, parotid duct injury, lid margin/canaliculus involvement, globe injury, cartilage exposure with inadequate cover, human/animal bite, gross contamination, delayed presentation >24h with infection, underlying fracture.

Antibiotics

Routine antibiotics NOT required for every facial laceration. Consider when: animal/human bite, gross contamination, open fracture, cartilage involvement, delayed presentation, immunocompromise. Follow local antimicrobial guidance.

Tetanus

Always assess tetanus status. Follow national guidance regarding boosters and immunoglobulin where indicated.

Dressing

Most facial wounds: simple non-adherent dressing for 24h. Some left uncovered. Avoid bulky dressings over face where possible.

Suture Removal

Scar Advice

Advise: keep wound clean, protect from sunlight 12+ months, SPF 50 sunscreen, silicone gel once epithelialised, gentle scar massage after healing. Explain scars mature for up to 1 year.

Key Takeaways

Examine before you anaesthetise and anaesthetise before you suture — nerve function must be documented pre-LA

Copious irrigation is one of the most important parts of wound management

Always assess facial nerve function before closure when wound is over cheek/parotid region

Vermilion border is the most important landmark in lip repair — align it first

Good cosmetic outcomes depend on assessment, layered closure, meticulous handling — not more stitches
