Clinical Emergency M39

Post-Operative Head & Neck Cancer Surgery

You are not expected to know everything. You are expected to recognise when something is wrong and get the right person involved early.

Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Post-operative head and neck cancer patients are among the most complex patients you will encounter on an OMFS/maxillofacial ward.

Major oral cavity or oropharyngeal resection

Composite resection and neck dissection

Free-flap reconstruction

Pedicled flap reconstruction

Tracheostomy

Total laryngectomy

Pharyngectomy

Mandibulectomy/maxillectomy

Skin resection and reconstruction

PEG insertion

Multiple drains

Complex vascular anastomoses

The patient can therefore deteriorate rapidly from airway compromise, haemorrhage, flap compromise, sepsis, aspiration, electrolyte disturbance, chyle leak or nutritional problems.

For the DCT, the key principle is:

"You do not need to manage these patients alone. You do need to recognise deterioration early, start appropriate immediate management and escalate promptly."

Learning Objectives

Identify the major components of a head and neck cancer operation.

Perform a structured post-operative assessment.

Recognise potentially life-threatening airway problems.

Understand the difference between a tracheostomy and a laryngectomy.

Perform and interpret basic free-flap observations.

Recognise early signs of flap compromise.

Identify post-operative neck haematoma and haemorrhage.

Recognise chyle leak.

Recognise salivary/pharyngeal fistula.

Assess wounds, drains and reconstruction.

Understand the principles of post-operative nutrition and fluid management.

Recognise sepsis, respiratory deterioration and thromboembolic disease.

Know when to contact the registrar, consultant, anaesthetics, ENT, ICU/HDU or other specialist teams.

Document an appropriate post-operative assessment and escalation plan.

The First Five Minutes at the Bedside

If called because the patient is unwell:

The key question:

"What has changed from this patient's previous baseline?"

The A–E Quick Check

A – AIRWAY — Is the patient talking? Is there stridor? Is there airway obstruction? Is the tracheostomy patent? Is the tube displaced? Is there blood/secretions obstructing the tube? Is this a laryngectomy patient?

B – BREATHING — Respiratory rate; oxygen saturations; work of breathing; chest movement; breath sounds; oxygen requirement.

C – CIRCULATION — Heart rate; blood pressure; capillary refill; peripheral perfusion; bleeding; drain output; neck swelling; urine output.

D – DISABILITY — Conscious level; confusion; pain; blood glucose if appropriate.

E – EXPOSURE — Neck; flap; wound; drains; donor site; tracheostomy; chest; abdomen; legs for DVT signs.

The Post-Operative Head & Neck Cancer Ward Round

A useful sequence is:

Airway → Flap → Neck → Wound → Drains → Nutrition → Fluids/electrolytes → Respiratory status → Pain → Mobility/VTE → Blood results → Antibiotics/medications → Rehabilitation → Discharge planning

Airway – Your First Priority

Head and neck cancer surgery can completely alter normal airway anatomy.

Oedema

Bleeding

Haematoma

Secretions

Mucus plugging

Tube obstruction

Tube displacement

False passage

Aspiration

Flap swelling

Pharyngeal swelling

Infection

Pneumonia

Never assume the airway is secure because the patient has a tracheostomy.

A tracheostomy can become blocked, displaced, decannulated, malpositioned, or obstructed by secretions or blood.

Tracheostomy vs Laryngectomy

This distinction is critical.

Tracheostomy

The patient usually retains a connection between the upper airway and lungs. Therefore, depending on the circumstances, oxygenation may potentially be achieved through the face, mouth/nose or tracheostomy.

Total laryngectomy

The patient's airway is permanently separated from the mouth and nose.

A laryngectomy patient breathes exclusively through the neck stoma.

DCT Survival Box

If you encounter a patient with a neck stoma in an emergency: "Find out immediately whether this is a tracheostomy or a laryngectomy." If uncertain, seek urgent senior/airway assistance.

Tracheostomy Emergency

If a tracheostomy patient deteriorates:

Call for help.

Assess ABC.

Give oxygen.

Assess the patient, not just the monitor.

Remove obvious obstruction if appropriate.

Check tube position and patency.

Suction if indicated.

Follow the patient's tracheostomy emergency algorithm/local protocol.

Involve anaesthetics/ENT/OMFS/critical care early where appropriate.

Red flags

Sudden desaturation

Increasing respiratory effort

No airflow through tube

Unable to pass suction catheter

High airway resistance

New bleeding

Tube displacement

Subcutaneous emphysema

Rapid neck swelling

A blocked or displaced tracheostomy is an airway emergency.

Follow your local tracheostomy emergency algorithm and obtain expert airway assistance early.

Free-Flap Monitoring

Free-flap reconstruction is one of the most important reasons these patients require close post-operative observation.

The flap depends on arterial inflow, venous drainage and patent microvascular anastomoses. Failure can result in flap loss.

Depending on the flap and local protocol, assess:

Colour

Temperature

Turgor

Capillary refill

Pin-prick bleeding where prescribed

Doppler signal

Swelling

Congestion

Comparison with previous observations

The most important principle:

"Know what the flap looked and sounded like previously."

A single observation is less useful than a change from baseline.

⚠ Flap Red Flags

What should the DCT do?

Escalate immediately. Do not wait until the next ward round. Do not assume that a flap with a marginal observation will "probably improve". Do not repeatedly manipulate the flap yourself while waiting for senior review.

DCT Survival Rule

"A potentially compromised free flap is a time-critical surgical problem."

Neck Dissection

After neck dissection, inspect for:

Haematoma

Seroma

Wound breakdown

Infection

Salivary leak

Chyle leak

Skin flap necrosis

Facial/neck swelling

Neurological deficit

Know whether major structures were preserved or sacrificed.

Possible post-operative neurological findings include shoulder weakness, tongue weakness, lower facial weakness, vocal cord dysfunction and sensory changes. The significance depends on the operative findings and structures involved.

Post-Operative Neck Haematoma

This is one of the most important complications to recognise.

Clinical features

Increasing neck swelling

Tight neck

Pain

Bruising

Increasing drain output or surprisingly little drain output

Dysphagia

Respiratory difficulty

Stridor

Venous congestion

Anxiety/restlessness

Tachycardia

Hypotension

Critical point:

A drain does not exclude a neck haematoma.

A drain may be blocked, kinked, displaced, too small to drain the collection, or positioned away from the bleeding point.

Management

Call senior surgical help immediately.

ABC assessment.

Oxygen.

IV access.

Bloods/crossmatch as appropriate.

Activate local major haemorrhage pathway if indicated.

Early anaesthetic/critical care involvement.

Prepare for urgent return to theatre if required.

DCT Survival Box

"Neck swelling + airway symptoms after major head and neck surgery = emergency until proven otherwise."

Instead document:

Number of drains

Location

Amount

Character

Trend

Suction status

Whether functioning

Chyle Leak

The thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct can be injured during neck dissection.

A chyle leak may present as:

Increasing drain output

Milky/creamy drainage

Increased output after feeding

Neck swelling

Fluid collection

Electrolyte disturbance

Protein loss

Nutritional depletion

Immunological compromise

Think about chyle leak when:

"Drain output becomes milky, particularly after enteral feeding."

Management depends on volume of leak, timing, patient condition, surgical anatomy and local protocol.

Treatment may involve dietary modification, specialist dietetic input, drain management, pharmacological treatment in selected cases, or surgical intervention for persistent/high-output leaks.

Do not independently change enteral feeding without discussing the plan with the surgical and dietetic teams.

Salivary Leak / Pharyngeal Fistula

Following oral/pharyngeal cancer surgery, leakage may occur from the oral cavity, pharynx, anastomosis or salivary gland region.

Look for:

Increasing drain output

Cloudy/salivary drainage

Wound wetness

Foul smell

Skin maceration

Neck swelling

Fever

Wound breakdown

A fistula can subsequently lead to infection, abscess, carotid exposure and wound breakdown.

DCT action

Escalate early. Do not simply document "possible saliva leak" and wait several days.

Assess:

Erythema

Wound separation

Skin necrosis

Flap viability

Odour

Check systemic features:

Fever

Tachycardia

Hypotension

Increasing oxygen requirement

A normal temperature does not exclude infection or sepsis.

Nutrition, Hydration and Swallowing

Major head and neck cancer surgery can significantly impair oral intake, swallowing, speech, taste, saliva and mastication.

Patients may require:

NG feeding

PEG feeding

RIG feeding

Modified diets

Thickened fluids

Speech and language therapy

Dietetic support

The DCT should know:

Is the patient NBM?

Why?

What is the feeding route?

What is the current feeding regimen?

Is the patient tolerating feeds?

Is there a dietetic plan?

Has SLT reviewed the patient?

Are medications being administered appropriately?

Is there an adequate fluid prescription?

Avoid the common mistake:

"Not eating" ≠ "nothing to worry about because they have a PEG."

Patients can still become dehydrated, malnourished, electrolyte depleted, hyperglycaemic, constipated or overloaded.

Refeeding Risk

Patients with prolonged poor intake may be at risk of refeeding syndrome.

Think about:

Phosphate

Potassium

Magnesium

Thiamine

Fluid balance

High-risk patients require appropriate nutritional assessment and monitoring according to local/NICE protocols.

DCT Survival Box

"Starting feeds is not simply a matter of prescribing calories. Know the patient's nutritional risk and follow the dietetic/medical plan."

VTE and Anticoagulation

These patients can have significant thromboembolic risk because of major cancer surgery, malignancy, reduced mobility, age/comorbidity and prolonged hospitalisation.

Check:

VTE assessment

Mechanical prophylaxis

Pharmacological prophylaxis

Renal function

Bleeding risk

Timing around surgery/procedures

But remember: head and neck cancer surgery also carries a significant bleeding risk.

Do not independently start, stop or alter anticoagulation without understanding the surgical plan and discussing it with the appropriate senior team.

Fluids and Electrolytes

These patients can become "quietly unwell".

Monitor:

Sodium

Potassium

Magnesium

Phosphate

Calcium

Urea

Creatinine

Glucose

Haemoglobin

Also assess:

Fluid balance

Urine output

Drain losses

Enteral losses

IV fluid administration

Particular attention: high-output drains can produce significant fluid and electrolyte losses.

Do not simply look at the blood results in isolation.

Ask:

"What has the patient lost, and what have we replaced?"

Pain

Good analgesia is important because poorly controlled pain can impair breathing, coughing, mobilisation, sleep and nutrition.

Review:

Site

Character

Analgesic requirements

Paracetamol

Sedation

Respiratory depression

Nausea

Constipation

A new change in behaviour or cognition should not simply be labelled "confused post-op".

Look for reversible causes:

Infection

Hypercapnia

Pain

Dehydration

DCT principle

"Do not focus exclusively on the surgical wound. The patient is more than the flap."

What Is the DCT's Job?

Recognise → Assess → Act → Escalate → Document → Reassess

Recognise: Identify abnormal findings.

Assess: Perform an appropriate clinical assessment.

Act: Start immediate appropriate management.

Escalate: Tell the registrar/consultant early.

Document: Record what you found, what you did and who you contacted.

Reassess: Do not assume that because someone has reviewed the patient, your responsibility to monitor them has ended.

When to Call the Senior Immediately

Consultant-level principle

"If you are worried enough to think 'I wonder if I should call the registrar', call the registrar."

The Post-Operative Handover

A good handover should include:

"This is a [age]-year-old patient, day X following [operation]."

Then:

Airway – tracheostomy/laryngectomy, tube details, current oxygen requirement.

Flap – type, viability, Doppler, any concerns.

Neck – wound, swelling, drain output.

Nutrition – oral/NG/PEG, feeding regimen, SLT/dietetic plan.

Bloods – Hb, WCC/CRP, renal function, electrolytes.

Current issues – infection, chyle, fistula, respiratory issue, pain, mobility.

Plan – what needs doing today, what needs monitoring and what requires escalation.

Documentation

A useful post-operative entry should allow another clinician to understand the patient's current status.

Discharge Planning Starts Early

Discharge is not simply: "Wound looks good — home."

Consider:

Airway – Is the patient decannulated? Is stoma care understood? Is equipment available?

Nutrition – Oral intake? NG? PEG? Feeding plan? Community dietetic support?

Wound – Dressings? Drain? Suture/staple plan?

Medication – Analgesia, antibiotics, anticoagulation and other regular medication.

Rehabilitation – SLT, dietetics, physiotherapy, OT and community support.

Follow-up – Surgical team, oncology, MDT, SLT, dietetics, restorative/prosthodontic services as required.

The Golden DCT Rule

When reviewing a complex post-operative head and neck cancer patient:

AIRWAY → FLAP → BLEEDING → SEPSIS → NUTRITION → FLUIDS → DRAINS → WOUND → RESPIRATORY STATUS → REHABILITATION

And remember:

"You are not expected to know everything. You are expected to recognise when something is wrong and get the right person involved early."

Key Take-Home Message

Major head and neck cancer surgery produces patients with multiple simultaneous potential points of failure.

The DCT's role is not to become the reconstructive surgeon, intensivist, airway specialist, dietitian and oncologist overnight.

Your role is to:

Recognise → Assess → Stabilise → Escalate → Document → Reassess

The most dangerous mistakes are often not failures of advanced surgical knowledge. They are:

Not recognising an airway problem

Not recognising a neck haematoma

Missing a compromised flap

Ignoring a change in drain output

Failing to recognise sepsis

Assuming a patient is well because their observations are initially normal

Failing to escalate early

SEE THE PATIENT. KNOW THE OPERATION. RECOGNISE THE CHANGE. CALL EARLY.
