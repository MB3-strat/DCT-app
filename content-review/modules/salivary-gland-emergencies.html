Clinical Emergency M29

Salivary Gland Emergencies

A painful swollen salivary gland is usually obstructed until proven otherwise. Your job is to distinguish obstruction, infection and abscess formation from neoplasia.

#salivary-gland#sialadenitis#sialolithiasis#parotid#submandibular#abscess#Ludwig-angina#airway#on-call#red-flag
Educational aid only. This is a personal reference and survival resource. It does not replace senior clinical advice, local trust policies, professional judgment, or emergency escalation procedures.

Why This Module Matters

Acute salivary gland swellings are core OMFS on-call work. Most are due to obstruction by sialoliths leading to stasis and secondary bacterial infection. The spectrum is wide: mild sialadenitis resolving with hydration, through to deep neck space abscesses and Ludwig’s angina that threaten airway. Neoplasia can mimic acute inflammation. Your job: identify the gland, assess airway/sepsis fast, decide if obstruction/infection/abscess, and know when admission or theatre is needed. Ludwig’s angina kills by airway obstruction, not sepsis — miss this and the patient dies.

Golden Rule

Sudden painful swelling of a salivary gland on eating is a stone until proven otherwise. Bilateral floor of mouth swelling + tongue elevation = airway emergency — act now, image later.

Key Anatomy

Initial Assessment – ABCDE

Airway: Priority. Ludwig’s angina = bilateral submandibular + sublingual + submental spaces. Signs: raised floor of mouth, tongue pushed up/back, drooling, “hot potato” voice, stridor. If present: stay with patient, sit upright, call anaesthetics + reg now. Do NOT send for CT or lie flat.

Breathing: RR, SpO2, work of breathing.

Circulation: Temp, HR, BP. Sepsis screen if NEWS2 ≥5 or looks unwell.

Disability: GCS, pain.

Exposure: Neck swelling, extent, skin erythema, trismus, cervical lymphadenopathy.

History – Key Questions

Timing: Seconds with food = stone. Hours-days worsening = infection. Weeks painless = neoplasm.

Pain: Colicky on eating = obstruction. Constant throbbing = infection/abscess.

Swelling: Intermittent = stone. Persistent + increasing = infection.

Systemic: Fever, rigors, malaise, dehydration.

PMH: Sjögren’s, HIV, previous radiotherapy, stones, anticholinergics, dehydration, diabetes, immunosuppression.

Dental: Poor OH, calculus, recent extraction.

Examination

Extraoral: Unilateral warm tender swelling over anatomical site. Bimanual palpation for submandibular gland: one finger intraoral floor of mouth, one extraoral. Stone often palpable in Wharton’s.

Intraoral: Inspect duct orifices. Look for erythema, swelling. Milk gland from posterior to anterior — pus confirms bacterial sialadenitis. Check dentition.

Trismus: Measure inter-incisal opening. <20mm suggests masticator space involvement.

Floor of Mouth: Raise tongue — if raised, woody, tender bilaterally = Ludwig’s until proven otherwise.

Cranial nerves: Facial nerve if parotid swelling — parotid malignancy can present acutely with infection.

Differential Diagnosis

Red Flags 🚩

Immediate senior/anaesthetics if: stridor, voice change, dysphagia, drooling, tongue elevation, trismus <20mm, rapidly spreading cellulitis, sepsis, immunocompromise, failure to improve 24–48h on IV antibiotics. These suggest deep space abscess or Ludwig’s.

Investigations

Bloods: FBC, CRP, U&E, blood cultures if febrile/septic.

Pus: MC&S if expressed or drained.

Imaging:

USS: First line. Differentiates sialadenitis vs abscess vs stone. Bedside, no radiation. Can guide aspiration.

OPG: Shows 80% of submandibular stones. Quick if available.

Contrast CT neck: For Ludwig’s, trismus preventing exam, deep abscess suspicion, failed treatment, or airway concern. Shows extent + airway.

MRI: Outpatient for suspected neoplasm, not emergency.

Management Pathways

1. Sialolithiasis + Mild Sialadenitis

Conservative: Hydration 2–3L/day, sialogogues — lemon, chewing gum, warm compress, gland massage toward orifice, analgesia, good OH. Antibiotics only if infection signs: co-amoxiclav 625mg TDS 5–7 days. Penicillin allergy: clindamycin 300mg QDS. Most small stones pass. OP follow-up for USS/sialendoscopy. Safety-net: return if fever, dysphagia, breathing issues.

2. Acute Bacterial Sialadenitis – Unwell

Admit if: unable to swallow, sepsis, large swelling, immunosuppressed. IV co-amoxiclav 1.2g TDS, IV fluids, analgesia, warm compress, massage. Reassess 24h with USS. If abscess → drainage.

3. Salivary Gland Abscess

Fluctuant, systemic illness, not settling on antibiotics. Confirm with USS. Needs drainage: intraoral if accessible, extraoral if deep parotid/submandibular. Send pus MC&S. Continue IV antibiotics 24–48h post-drainage then oral. Ludwig’s angina = bilateral spaces → theatre for wide drainage + airway.

4. Ludwig’s Angina Protocol

Call anaesthetics + senior OMFS/ENT immediately.

Sit patient up. High-flow O2. Do NOT lie flat.

Secure airway: awake fibreoptic intubation or tracheostomy. Intubation difficult due to oedema.

CT only AFTER airway safe.

Theatre: bilateral submandibular + submental decompression.

IV antibiotics: co-amoxiclav + metronidazole, or per local severe deep neck space protocol.

ITU post-op. Mortality high if airway missed.

Antibiotics

Cover S. aureus, oral streps, anaerobes. Pasteurella not usual here.

First line: IV co-amoxiclav 1.2g TDS. PO: 625mg TDS.

Penicillin allergy: clindamycin 600mg IV TDS or 300mg PO QDS + metronidazole 400mg TDS.

Adjust to cultures. Duration: 7–14 days total depending on drainage.

Admission Criteria

Airway concern, Ludwig’s, unable to take oral meds/fluids, sepsis, abscess needing drainage, immunocompromised, failed outpatient Rx, no social support, child.

Discharge Criteria

Well, tolerating oral intake, pain controlled, no airway issues, clear plan + follow-up, safety-netted. Warn: return if swelling ↑, swallowing/breathing worse, fever persists >48h on antibiotics, trismus worsens.

Registrar Handover Prompt

“28F, 6h R submandibular pain/swelling on eating lunch. Temp 38.1, NEWS2 2. Tender R submandibular gland, 5mm stone palpable in Wharton’s duct, pus expressed. FOM soft, no trismus, voice normal. USS: distal stone, no abscess. Impression: acute obstructive sialadenitis. Plan: IV co-amoxiclav, hydration, analgesia, milk duct. Review AM for stone removal vs conservative. Safety-netted.”

Airway version: “62M, 48h worsening bilateral submandibular swelling. Drooling, muffled voice, FOM raised and tender, tongue elevated. RR 22, SpO2 94%. Stridor absent but concerned. Impression: Ludwig’s angina. Anaesthetics + ITU called, NBM, IV co-amoxiclav + metronidazole, for emergency airway + theatre drainage.”

DCT Survival Box 🚨 The Three Questions That Save Lives

Is the airway safe? Check voice, swallow, FOM, tongue. If no, stop and escalate.

Is it infected or just blocked? Pus + fever = infection. Meal timing = stone.

Do I need to drain or scan now? Fluctuance/trismus/systemic = USS/CT + drainage.

If airway unsafe, nothing else matters until anaesthetics are present.

DCT Survival Box 🚨 – The "5 Ms" of Salivary Gland Assessment

Before presenting any salivary gland patient, run through:

Meals — Does swelling worsen when eating? Stone/obstruction if yes

Massage — Does saliva or pus express from the duct? Pus = bacterial sialadenitis

Mass — Is it diffuse, fluctuant or a discrete lump? Diffuse = sialadenitis, fluctuant = abscess, discrete lump = ?tumour

Movement — Is facial nerve function normal? Parotid malignancy can present with facial weakness

Markers — What do inflammatory markers and imaging show? WCC/CRP ↑ = infection, USS/CT = stone vs abscess vs mass

Answer these five questions and you’ll have covered the key clinical issues in almost every salivary gland emergency.
