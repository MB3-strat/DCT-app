Welcome to OMFS

The first job of the OMFS DCT is not to know everything—it is to recognise the sick patient, provide safe initial management, and know when to ask for help.

Why This Matters

Starting an OMFS post can feel daunting. Unlike dental practice, OMFS combines dentistry, medicine and surgery. Within a single shift you may assess facial trauma, drain an odontogenic infection, review a tracheostomy, assist in theatre, manage ward patients, and receive referrals from emergency departments, general dental practitioners, GPs and other hospital specialties. No one expects a new DCT to know everything on day one. They do expect you to be organised, systematic, safe, and willing to escalate concerns early.

Golden Rule

Airway always comes first. A patient with a dental infection can deteriorate rapidly if the airway becomes compromised.

Core Content

What is OMFS?

Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (OMFS) is the surgical specialty concerned with the diagnosis and management of diseases, injuries and abnormalities affecting the mouth, teeth, jaws, face, neck, salivary glands, facial skin, and oral mucosa.

Most UK OMFS units also contribute to the management of: facial trauma, head and neck cancer, deep neck space infections, complex dentoalveolar surgery, orthognathic surgery, cleft surgery, craniofacial surgery, facial deformity, TMJ surgery, skin cancer.

Your Role as a DCT

What Your Consultant Expects

Consultants do not expect you to know every diagnosis. They do expect you to: be punctual, be polite, know your patients, prioritise safety, escalate concerns promptly, keep accurate documentation, be honest if you do not know something.

The Golden Rules of OMFS

Assess the patient—not the X-ray. Never let imaging replace a thorough history and examination.

Airway always comes first. A patient with a dental problem can wait. A patient with an airway problem cannot.

Pain rarely kills. Airway obstruction, sepsis and haemorrhage do. Learn to identify immediately life-threatening problems before focusing on pain relief.

Never be afraid to escalate. Registrars and consultants would rather receive ten unnecessary calls than miss one patient with impending airway compromise.

Good documentation protects patients. If it is not documented, it may be assumed not to have happened.

Is the patient stable? Assess airway, breathing, circulation

Why has OMFS been called? Clarify exact reason: swelling, trauma, bleeding, dental pain, oral lesion

Does this patient need immediate review? Airway compromise, active haemorrhage, visual disturbance, neurological deficit, sepsis

What information do I need before I attend? Referrer name, patient details, location, observations, imaging, blood results, referrer's concern

Consultant Pearls 💡

The best DCTs are not necessarily the most knowledgeable—they are the most reliable

Always introduce yourself to nursing staff. They will often recognise deterioration before anyone else

Review imaging yourself before discussing it with a senior but never rely on the image alone

Key Takeaways

Patient safety always comes first — airway, sepsis and haemorrhage are your priority emergencies

Good documentation and communication are as important as clinical knowledge

Your role is safe initial assessment — begin appropriate management and seek senior support when needed

Registrar Handover Prompt

Before phoning the registrar, be ready to answer: Who is the patient? Why have they been referred? What are the observations? Is the airway safe? What does the examination show? What investigations have been performed? What is your working diagnosis? What management have you started? What do you want from the registrar?
